Why the agreement you skip is the one that sinks you

The good times are exactly when you should write down the bad ones

Take two friends launching a bookkeeping SaaS together. Year one, they split everything 50/50 on a handshake, no problem, they're both coding every evening. Year two, one of them goes part-time to care for a parent, then leaves entirely for a salaried job, but keeps the full 50% equity because nobody ever wrote a vesting schedule. The one who stays is now doing all the work for half the company, and there's no document that says otherwise. Nobody acted in bad faith. It's just what happens when nothing gets written down while things are fine.

That gap is exactly what a founders' agreement is for, and you're not the only one reaching for AI to write it. Over half of small firms and solo practitioners are already using generative AI in their legal work, so treating a chatbot as a serious first drafter isn't a shortcut, it's fairly normal by now.

How to draft one with AI in an evening

Five terms to nail down, then a lawyer just checks your homework

Open ChatGPT or Claude and describe your situation in plain English: how many founders, the current equity split, who's full-time versus part-time, and what should happen if someone leaves. Ask it to draft a founders' agreement covering five things specifically: equity split, a four-year vesting schedule with a one-year cliff, roles and decision-making, leaver terms (good leaver versus bad leaver), and IP assignment, confirming that everything built for the company belongs to the company, not to whoever's laptop it was written on.

Then push back on the draft the way you'd push back on a junior associate: ask what's unusual for a UK company your size, and what a generic template usually gets wrong. Take that draft, not a blank page, to a solicitor for a fixed-fee review. You're paying for judgment on the edge cases, not for someone to type out clauses you already knew you needed.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Can AI actually write a legally binding founders' agreement?

The draft isn't binding until you and your co-founders sign it, and a UK solicitor should still check the final version. What AI is genuinely good for is turning a blank page into a structured first draft covering the terms that matter, which is most of the actual work.

What's the single term most first-time founders forget?

Vesting. Without it, someone can walk away in month three and keep their full slice of equity forever. A standard four-year schedule with a one-year cliff protects everyone still doing the work.

Do we need this if we're still pre-revenue and just two friends?

Especially then. The agreement is cheapest and easiest to write while you're getting on well. Every month you wait, whatever's unwritten just becomes whatever anyone remembers it to be.

How much should a solicitor's review actually cost?

Ask for a fixed fee to review an AI-drafted agreement rather than an hourly rate to write one from scratch. Most UK startup solicitors will quote a flat rate once there's already a draft to work from.

Why this matters for you

For a very small company, a founders' dispute isn't a boardroom problem, it's an existential one. There's no HR department to mediate, no board to break a tie, and usually not enough cash reserve to survive months of the two people who run the business not speaking to each other. A written agreement doesn't prevent disagreements, but it turns them into a five-minute look at a document instead of a slow-motion argument about who was right, which is often the difference between a bump in the road and the whole thing folding.
