Why Most Pilots Never Pay Off

MIT's research on the "GenAI Divide"

MIT's 2025 study of AI deployments across 300 public rollouts, 350 employees, and 150 leadership interviews found that 95% of generative AI pilots fail to move a company's profit and loss in any measurable way. Only around 5% achieve real, rapid impact.

The cause MIT identifies is organisational, not technical: what the researchers call a learning gap, where companies never actually fold the tool into how people work day to day. It stays a side project. It never becomes part of the job.

One detail matters for smaller businesses in particular: buying a tool from a specialised vendor and working with a partner to implement it succeeds roughly 67% of the time. Building the same thing entirely in-house succeeds about a third as often. Small teams rarely have the spare capacity to build well, so this is good news, not bad news, for businesses without an in-house data team.

The Same Problem, Smaller Scale

What S&P Global found across 1,000+ enterprises applies just as much to a five-person team

S&P Global surveyed more than 1,000 enterprises across North America and Europe and found that 42% of them abandoned most of their AI initiatives in 2025, up sharply from 17% the year before. On average, organisations scrapped 46% of their AI proofs of concept before anything reached production. The top reasons given were cost, and concerns over data privacy and security.

For a small business, this looks like signing up for two or three AI tools, using them inconsistently for a few weeks, and quietly letting the subscriptions lapse without ever writing down what they were supposed to achieve. The scale is smaller. The pattern is identical.

What the UK Data Shows

The Federation of Small Businesses surveyed 904 owners in late 2025

The FSB's most recent survey found AI adoption among UK small businesses has reached around 54 to 55%, up from roughly 20% in 2023. Among those using it, 59% report a productivity gain, 24% report higher revenue, and 22% report business growth, with an average revenue uplift of about 3%.

The results are real, and so is the discomfort. 92% of small business owners say they are worried about AI-related risks, up from 73% two years ago, with concerns centred on inaccurate outputs, data security, and unclear liability. Adoption and anxiety are rising together, which is exactly what happens when a business starts using a tool before it has a way to check whether that tool is actually working.

Quick Answers

What small business owners ask us most

Is a 95% pilot failure rate really relevant to a small business?

The MIT research covers enterprises, but the root cause, tools that never get folded into daily work, applies at any size. A small business just has fewer people for the habit to spread through, which can make it easier to fix once it's named.

How long before we should expect to see a return?

For a single, well-scoped workflow, two to four weeks is usually enough to see whether your chosen metric has moved. If it hasn't moved by then, the workflow or the tool needs changing, not more patience.

Should we build something custom or buy an existing tool?

For most small businesses, buying from a specialised vendor and getting help implementing it well outperforms building in-house, by a wide margin according to MIT's data. Custom builds make sense once you know exactly what you need, not before.

What should we measure first?

Something you already track: hours on a task, response time, cost per job, or a similar operational number. Revenue and growth metrics matter, but they move too slowly and are affected by too many other things to prove a single workflow works.

Why this matters for you

If you're running a small business, you don't have a data science team to catch a failing AI pilot before it costs you six months and a subscription budget.
