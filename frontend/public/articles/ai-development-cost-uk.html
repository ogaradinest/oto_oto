The question isn't really "how much does AI cost". It's "cost over what period". A subscription tool and a custom build can serve the exact same workflow, and still have completely different cost curves, one cheap now and expensive later, the other the reverse. Knowing which shape your spending is about to take matters more than any single price tag.

The true total cost of off-the-shelf AI tools runs 2.5 to 4 times the advertised subscription price once you count seat surcharges, usage tiers, and the AI-specific fee that's now standard. That gap is where budgets quietly blow past plan.

Off-the-shelf: cheap to start, expensive forever

The subscription model has a hidden multiplier

Off-the-shelf tools with AI features typically run £40 to £400 per user, per month, and the AI-enabled tiers are almost never on the cheapest plan. Most small businesses can get an AI tool running for under £4,000 in setup, or £16 to £80 a month per user, on the surface.

The catch is that AI feature surcharges have risen an average of 34% since 2024, and once you add usage-based overage charges and per-seat scaling, actual total cost of ownership runs 2.5 to 4 times the number on the pricing page. A tool that looked like £50 a month at signup can genuinely cost £150 to £200 a month once your team and usage grow into it.

Custom builds: expensive to start, cheap forever

The inverse cost curve

Custom AI development runs roughly £3,000 to £13,500 for a focused single-task tool, £6,500 to £33,000 for a multi-system workflow automation, and £20,000 to £120,000 for a full platform build. A simple single-workflow agent typically lands at £12,000 to £32,000, broadly consistent with the reactive and intermediate tiers covered in our AI agent development cost guide.

The median cost of a custom AI project for a mid-market business sits around £69,000, with ongoing annual maintenance running 15 to 25% of that original build cost. Unlike a subscription, that maintenance cost stays relatively flat as your usage grows, which is exactly why the two cost curves eventually cross.

Where the two curves actually cross

A concrete break-even point, not a vague "it depends"

For a small team, break-even between subscribing and building typically lands at 12 to 24 months. A more precise rule of thumb: a £120,000 custom build tends to pay for itself in around 15 months once your combined SaaS and API spend passes roughly £8,000 a month. Below that spend level, subscribing almost always wins on cost. Above it, sustained for more than a year, building starts to make financial sense.

The decision rule worth actually using

One question that resolves most build-vs-buy debates

If an off-the-shelf tool covers 80% or more of your workflow as it stands, buy it. The remaining 20% is rarely worth a custom build on its own. If your combined subscription and usage-based AI spend is already above roughly £4,000 a month and climbing, that's the signal to run the build-versus-buy maths properly, rather than continuing to add subscriptions by default.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Is custom AI development ever worth it for a small business?

Yes, once subscription and usage costs for the equivalent off-the-shelf tools are consistently high and growing. Below that threshold, subscribing remains the lower-risk, lower-cost option for most small businesses.

Why do subscription AI tools get so much more expensive than advertised?

Usage-based pricing tiers, per-seat charges that scale with your team, and AI-specific feature surcharges that sit on top of the base subscription. All three tend to be underweighted when comparing the advertised price to a custom build quote.

Does the maintenance cost on a custom build ever go away?

No, budget for it as a permanent line item, typically 15 to 25% of the original build cost every year, covering monitoring, retraining, and adjustments as your data and processes change.

What's the simplest first step if I'm unsure which path fits?

Add up your actual current spend on AI-enabled subscriptions across your business, including every seat and every usage overage. That real number, not the advertised one, is what any build-versus-buy comparison should start from.

Why this matters for you

Most small businesses default to subscriptions because the upfront number is smaller, without ever tracking whether that number is quietly growing past the point where a custom build would have been cheaper. The break-even math above isn't an argument for building custom software, it's a way to notice when your subscription stack has grown enough to be worth checking.
