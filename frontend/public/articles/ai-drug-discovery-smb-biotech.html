What Is Actually Getting Faster, and What Isn't

Discovery compresses, trials do not

The dramatic timeline reductions reported in AI drug discovery, in some cases cutting years to months, apply almost entirely to the earliest stages: identifying a biological target, generating and screening candidate molecules computationally, and predicting which candidates are worth synthesising. Preclinical candidate development has moved from a typical 3 to 4 years down to 13 to 18 months in AI-assisted pipelines.

Clinical trials themselves are not compressing at anything like the same rate. Regulatory review, patient recruitment and safety monitoring still take the time they take. A smaller biotech should treat AI as compressing the expensive, speculative front end of a programme, not as a shortcut through clinical development.

Why This Matters More for a Small Biotech's Cash Runway

The cost reduction lands earliest, where cash is tightest

AI adoption is reported to cut preclinical R&D costs by 25 to 50%, and industry estimates now put the average all-in cost of bringing a drug to market under $1.5 billion, down from a historical benchmark near $2.6 billion, with AI-integrated pipelines driving a meaningful share of that reduction. For a large pharma company, this improves margins across a huge portfolio. For a small biotech running on a fixed runway of venture funding, the same reduction determines whether a single programme survives to its next funding round.

The practical implication is to prioritise AI tools at the target identification and lead optimisation stage first, where the cost per experiment is highest relative to a small company's total budget, rather than waiting for an all-encompassing platform that covers the entire pipeline.

What MHRA Is Actually Doing to Support Smaller Players

Regulatory sandboxes, not just enterprise guidance

MHRA has committed to publishing a dedicated regulatory framework for AI as a medical device during 2026, and has pledged to run all requested "innovation surgeries", direct sessions helping smaller companies and academic teams navigate the regulatory pathway, by April 2027. This is a deliberate attempt to stop regulatory complexity being a barrier that only well-resourced companies can clear.

The Department of Health and Social Care has also committed £1.2 million a year through 2029 to the AI Airlock testing programme, moving it away from annual budget uncertainty and toward longer-term regulatory pathway development. A small biotech developing an AI-assisted diagnostic or discovery tool should engage with these programmes early, since they are explicitly designed to reduce the cost of regulatory navigation for exactly this kind of company.

A Realistic Way to Start Without a Nine-Figure Budget

Rent the capability before building it

Most smaller biotechs and CROs access AI drug discovery capability through partnerships and pay-as-you-go computational platforms rather than building proprietary models in-house. This avoids the upfront infrastructure cost while still capturing the target identification and screening speed gains that make the biggest difference to a constrained budget.

The sensible sequence is to pilot AI-assisted screening on a single target or programme first, measure the actual time and cost saved against your historical baseline, and only consider a deeper platform investment once that specific gain is demonstrated on your own data rather than assumed from industry-wide figures.

The questions founders ask most

Does AI actually shorten the whole drug development process?

Mostly the earliest stages: target identification and candidate screening, where preclinical timelines have moved from 3-4 years to roughly 13-18 months in AI-assisted pipelines. Clinical trials and regulatory review are not compressing at the same rate.

Can a small biotech access this without a huge budget?

Yes, largely through partnerships and pay-as-you-go computational platforms rather than building models in-house. This captures most of the cost and time benefit at the discovery stage without the infrastructure investment large pharma companies make.

What is MHRA doing to help smaller companies with AI-related products?

Publishing a dedicated regulatory framework for AI as a medical device in 2026, and committing to run all requested innovation surgeries for SMEs and academic teams by April 2027, alongside continued funding for the AI Airlock testing programme through 2029.

Where should a cash-constrained biotech focus its first AI investment?

Target identification and lead optimisation, where AI-driven cost reductions of 25 to 50% have the largest proportional impact on a limited runway, rather than a broader platform covering the entire pipeline.

Why this matters for you

For a venture-funded biotech or a contract research organisation, AI's biggest impact is not headline drug-approval speed, it is reducing the cost of the earliest, most speculative stage of a programme, exactly where a limited runway is most exposed. Accessing that capability through partnerships rather than in-house infrastructure, and engaging MHRA's SME-focused support early, brings pharma-scale discovery tools within reach of a company without a pharma-scale budget.
