The volume and workforce squeeze behind this conversation

More prescriptions, fewer pharmacies, fewer staff

Community pharmacies in England dispensed 1.16 billion prescription items in 2024/25, with per-pharmacy volume up 30% over the past six years. Over the same period the network has been shrinking rather than growing: more than 650 pharmacies closed in the last 12 months alone, the highest number in a single year for two decades, leaving the pharmacy network in England at its smallest size since 2006.

The staff who remain report the pressure directly: 88% of pharmacy staff cite workload as a top pressure, and 95% say staffing shortages are increasing daily strain. 60% of pharmacy teams report active staffing shortages, and over half of pharmacy owners say they are struggling to recruit permanent staff, a pattern reflected in England's projected shortfall of 16,000 community pharmacists by 2036/37.

Where AI is actually being applied: stock and workflow, not clinical judgement

Predictive ordering and dispensing throughput

The most established AI application in community pharmacy is stock forecasting: predictive ordering systems that analyse historical dispensing patterns, seasonal trends and prescription volume to anticipate what a pharmacy will need, reducing both wasted stock and last-minute shortages. This directly targets a cost pressure that compounds for a business already reporting financial strain, with at least 65% of English pharmacies estimated to have operated at a loss in 2025.

A second application targets dispensing throughput itself: incremental automation, starting with counting and inventory tools and expanding toward workflow and robotics as volume grows, aimed specifically at pharmacies reaching a throughput ceiling where manual processes can no longer keep pace with prescription volume. AI is also being used to redirect routine phone queries, freeing pharmacist and dispensing staff time for the clinical work they are trained and regulated to do.

What this means for a smaller, independent pharmacy

The same volume pressure, with less room to absorb it

An independent pharmacy facing 30% higher per-location dispensing volume than six years ago, with a smaller and harder-to-recruit workforce, has less capacity to absorb inefficient stock ordering or manual counting processes than a larger chain with centralised buying power. Predictive stock forecasting tools built for this specific pressure can reduce both the waste from over-ordering and the disruption from stockouts, without touching any clinical decision about a specific prescription or patient.

The financial case matters here specifically because of how thin margins already are: with an estimated 65% of pharmacies operating at a loss in 2025, a stock or workflow tool only needs to protect a modest amount of wasted spend or staff time to be worth its cost for a business already under this level of pressure.

A sensible way to evaluate a stock or workflow tool

Separate admin automation from anything touching clinical judgement

Before adopting any AI tool in a pharmacy setting, get a specific written answer on exactly what the tool does: forecasting stock levels and flagging reorder points is a different category from anything suggesting a clinical action, a dosage check, or medicines advice, and should be evaluated with a correspondingly lower bar for scrutiny.

Measure the tool against your own numbers before committing further: track wasted stock and stockout incidents for a month before adoption, then compare against the same period after, rather than relying on a vendor's industry-wide average. A tool addressing a genuine, measurable problem in your own pharmacy is a safer investment than one addressing a problem the industry has on average.

Why this matters for you

An independent pharmacy faces the same volume growth and workforce shortage driving the wider closure numbers, with far less buying power or centralised stock management than a chain. Predictive stock forecasting and workflow automation address a cost and time pressure that is proportionally larger for a smaller operation, without requiring the pharmacy to change anything about how clinical decisions are made or who is accountable for them.
