What changed to make this available to small businesses

The metering barrier that used to lock people out

Flexibility markets, where a business gets paid for reducing or shifting electricity use at times the grid needs it, used to be effectively restricted to large industrial users with half-hourly metering. Ofgem's 2026 changes removed that barrier for sites with standard, non-half-hourly meters, opening access to an estimated 345,000 additional households and small businesses through aggregators known as Virtual Lead Parties.

In practice, this means a business with any flexible electricity use, refrigeration that can run slightly off-peak, battery storage, EV charging, non-time-critical machinery, can now sign up through an aggregator and get paid for participating, without installing industrial-grade metering first.

Why AI is the part that makes this worth doing

Nobody wants to manually respond to a grid signal

The actual mechanism of flexibility services, responding to a signal that asks you to reduce or shift demand within a specific window, is not something a business owner wants to manage by hand. AI-based automation is what most aggregators use to detect the signal, decide how much load can be shifted without disrupting operations, and execute the change automatically, then verify it happened for settlement purposes.

This is also where the revenue actually becomes worthwhile at small scale: a manual response might catch a handful of high-value events a year, while automated, AI-managed participation captures events reliably enough that the numbers add up over a full year rather than occasionally.

What Ofgem now expects from AI use in the energy sector

New guidance, plus a sandbox for testing

Ofgem published its first formal guidance on ethical AI use in the energy sector in 2026, covering governance, accountability, risk assessment and organisational competence, aimed at licensees and the wider industry rather than end-use businesses. It is also launching a 12-month AI technical sandbox pilot in late autumn 2026, giving energy businesses a controlled environment to test AI use cases with regulatory input before wider rollout.

For a small business simply signing up to a flexibility service through an aggregator, this guidance applies to the aggregator, not to you directly. It is still worth checking that any aggregator you use can point to how it meets Ofgem's governance expectations, since that is a reasonable proxy for how carefully it is managing the automated decisions being made on your account.

For businesses that own energy assets directly

Predictive maintenance is the other AI use case worth knowing about

If your business owns or operates generation or storage assets directly, solar, battery storage, a wind asset, rather than simply participating as a flexible electricity consumer, AI-based predictive maintenance is the more relevant use case. Reported results include up to 60% reductions in offshore wind downtime and £4,000 to £15,000 saved per turbine per day in avoided outages, achieved by detecting mechanical degradation from sensor data weeks before failure.

For a smaller operator, the practical version of this rarely means building a custom monitoring system. It means checking whether your existing asset management or SCADA platform already includes a predictive maintenance module, since most modern platforms do, before considering a separate investment.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Do I need special metering to join a flexibility service now?

No, not any more for most small businesses. Ofgem's 2026 changes opened access to sites with standard, non-half-hourly metering through aggregators, removing a barrier that previously required expensive industrial-grade metering.

How does AI actually factor into a flexibility service?

Aggregators use AI to detect grid signals, decide how much load your business can safely shift, and automate the response, since manually reacting to these signals in real time is not practical for most businesses.

Does Ofgem's new AI guidance apply to my small business directly?

Only indirectly. The guidance targets licensees and industry participants like aggregators and suppliers. As a small business signing up for a flexibility service, it is reasonable to ask your aggregator how it meets that guidance rather than needing to comply with it yourself.

Is predictive maintenance relevant if we don't own energy infrastructure?

Not directly. It matters mainly for businesses that own generation, storage or significant mechanical assets. If you are simply a business using electricity, the flexibility market and automated demand response are the more relevant AI use case.

Why this matters for you

Most small businesses assume flexibility markets and energy AI are only relevant to utility companies. The 2026 metering changes specifically opened this market to smaller sites, and AI-managed automation through an aggregator is what makes participating worthwhile without anyone watching a dashboard. For a business with any flexible electricity use, refrigeration, storage, EV charging, non-urgent machinery, this is a genuinely passive revenue stream that did not exist in a practical form before this year.
