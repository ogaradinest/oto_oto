Decide the real rules before you write anything

AI can't guess your return window, who pays postage, or what's excluded

Before you touch ChatGPT or Claude, answer four questions on paper: how many days will you give customers to return something, who pays the postage, does the item need to be unused and in original packaging, and are any items excluded, such as made-to-order, perishable, personalised, or opened cosmetics.

These choices are yours to make as the business owner, not the AI's. A print-on-demand t-shirt seller and a bakery selling custom cakes need completely different rules, and a generic template pulled off Google tends to split the difference badly, covering neither one properly.

Draft with AI, then check it against UK law

Two legal baselines apply whether or not you wrote them down yourself

Feed your four decisions into ChatGPT, Claude, or a dedicated generator like iubenda, and ask for a return and refund policy written in plain English for your specific product and delivery setup. You'll have a working draft in minutes.

Then check it covers two things UK law already gives customers, regardless of your own rules: the 14-day cancellation right for most online orders under the Consumer Contracts Regulations, and the right to a full refund within 30 days for anything that arrives faulty under the Consumer Rights Act 2015. Your own policy can be more generous than these. It can't be stingier.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Do I legally need a returns policy for my small business?

There's no single law forcing every business to publish one, but UK consumer law already governs returns and refunds whether you've written anything down or not. Publishing your own policy just makes the rules clear upfront, rather than leaving customers to find out mid-dispute.

Can I refuse returns altogether?

For faulty goods, no, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 gives customers a refund or repair right regardless of your policy. For change-of-mind on most online orders, customers also get a 14-day statutory cancellation right you can't remove, though you can ask them to cover return postage.

Is an AI-drafted return policy good enough, or do I need a solicitor?

For most straightforward retail or ecommerce setups, an AI draft checked against the two UK baselines above is a solid working policy. If you sell regulated goods, subscriptions, or digital downloads with unusual terms, it's worth a solicitor's quick review before you publish.

How specific should my return window be?

Specific enough that a customer never has to ask. State the number of days, who pays postage, the condition goods must arrive in, and any excluded categories like personalised or perishable items, right on the policy page itself.

For a business launching on a tight budget, a clear return policy isn't just a legal box to tick, it's a sales tool.
