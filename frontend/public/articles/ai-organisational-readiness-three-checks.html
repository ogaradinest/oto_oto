MIT's NANDA initiative analysed 300 real generative AI deployments, alongside 150 leader interviews and a 350-employee survey, and found that only around 5% of pilots produced a measurable, rapid return. The other 95% stalled, with no real impact on the numbers that matter. Gartner has independently projected that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027, a separate figure pointing at the same underlying pattern. MIT's researchers were specific about the cause: the gap was rarely about the quality of the AI model itself. It came down to how the tool was chosen and who actually owned it once it launched. Three concrete traits separated the working pilots from the rest, and none of them require a formal AI vision statement, a governance board, or a learning programme to check.

Check One: Are You Buying or Building?

The gap is wider than most owners expect

MIT's data shows a stark split: buying a specialist tool and forming a real relationship with the vendor, one where support and adjustments actually happen, succeeded around 67% of the time. Building an equivalent system in-house succeeded roughly a third as often. This is not a stated preference from a survey. It is drawn from actual deployment outcomes. Unless a business already runs its own engineering team, this single check settles the build-versus-buy question in most cases before any comparison of specific vendors even starts.

Check Two: Is the Money Going Where the Return Actually Is?

Sales and marketing get the budget, back office gets the result

More than half of AI budgets in MIT's dataset went toward sales and marketing tools, content generation, lead scoring, and similar customer-facing applications. The strongest measured return, though, concentrated in back-office automation: cutting external agency and outsourcing costs, and streamlining routine processing work nobody outside the business ever sees. Before funding the most visible AI project, a readiness check worth running is whether a duller back-office task, invoicing, scheduling, or document processing, would actually pay back faster.

Check Three: Who Actually Owns It, Day to Day?

A committee is not the same as an owner

MIT's researchers found successful deployments were driven by the line managers and employees actually doing the work, not by a central AI committee overseeing things from a distance. The practical readiness check: name one specific person, not a department or a board, who will use the tool daily, notice when its output is wrong, and decide whether to keep using it. If nobody fits that description yet, the project is not ready to start, whatever the pilot's business case looks like on paper.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

What percentage of AI pilots actually deliver a measurable return?

Around 5%, according to MIT's analysis of 300 real enterprise AI deployments. The other 95% stalled with no measurable impact on revenue or costs, mainly due to how the tool was chosen and owned rather than the underlying model's quality.

Should a small business build its own AI tool or buy one?

MIT's data shows buying a specialist tool and pairing it with a genuine vendor relationship succeeded around 67% of the time, versus roughly a third as often for in-house builds. For most businesses without a dedicated engineering team, that is a strong steer toward buying.

Where does AI actually pay back fastest for a typical business?

MIT found back-office automation, cutting outsourced costs and streamlining routine processing, delivered a stronger measured return than the sales and marketing tools that receive the majority of AI budgets.

Do we need an AI committee or governance board before starting a project?

Not to start a first project. MIT's research found successful deployments were driven by the specific line manager or employee using the tool daily, not a central board. A named, hands-on owner matters more at the start than a formal governance structure.

Why this matters for you

A small business cannot absorb a 95% pilot failure rate the way a large enterprise budget can. Getting these three checks right before spending anything matters far more when there is no second budget line to fall back on. None of the checks need a consultant or a governance process, just honest answers to three specific questions before signing anything.
