The FSB found that 51% of small construction businesses believe AI is not appropriate for their business, the most sceptical response of any sector it tracks. Houzz's inaugural 2026 UK State of AI in Construction and Design Report, surveying over 145 industry professionals, found something closer to the opposite in practice: 46% already use AI, saving an average of more than three hours a week, worth over £23,000 a year in productivity per business. The two findings are not really a contradiction. Houzz's own breakdown shows adoption concentrated in admin work, 89% of construction professionals, and sales and marketing, 67%, while planning, design and takeoff work sits at just 33%. Free AI tools have already earned their place in construction businesses. Just not yet for the number that actually goes into a tender.

The adoption gap is really a task gap

What Houzz's numbers show underneath the headline figure

Houzz's survey breaks adoption down by task, and the pattern is consistent: 89% of construction professionals use AI for administrative work such as note-taking, formatting, and document editing, 67% for sales and marketing tasks like social content and lead management, and only 33% for planning, design and takeoff work, the category that eventually feeds into a priced tender. Free general-purpose AI tools are already doing real, measurable work in construction businesses. That work is heavily weighted towards the lowest-stakes categories.

CIOB's Skills Gap Index, based on responses from more than 2,500 members in autumn 2024, found 54% see a significant skills gap specifically in using AI, alongside 48% seeing gaps in digital technology more broadly. Put together with the FSB's 51% scepticism figure, a clearer picture emerges: it is not that AI does not work for construction, it is that the confidence and skill to use it for the number that matters most, the cost figure, still lags well behind the confidence to use it for admin and marketing.

Why the cost figure needs a different standard

Professional indemnity insurance does not treat these the same

Lockton's guidance for construction professionals is specific: AI-generated cost figures used in a tender without senior quantity surveyor validation may breach the terms of a professional indemnity policy, since insurers increasingly expect disclosure of how AI was used and may add exclusions relating to emerging technology. This is not a reason to avoid AI in estimating altogether. It is a reason to treat the cost figure differently from the admin task that produced the draft it sits inside.

A two-tier way to use free AI tools in estimating

Where the 33% should grow, and where a human stays in charge

Use free general-purpose tools freely for the estimating-adjacent admin work: parsing specifications, extracting dimensions from drawings, structuring a preliminary quantity list, drafting scope descriptions. This is exactly the category Houzz's data shows already working well and safely. Keep a senior quantity surveyor checking any actual cost figure before it goes into a tender, cross-referenced against a proper cost database rather than accepted from a general-purpose chatbot unsupervised. That single distinction, between drafting structure and confirming a number, is what keeps a free AI tool inside the boundary of what a PI policy will actually cover.

The questions small business owners ask most

Is AI actually useful for a small construction business, given how many owners are sceptical?

Yes, for specific tasks. Houzz's 2026 UK survey found 46% of construction professionals already use AI, concentrated in admin work (89%) and sales and marketing (67%). The FSB's finding that 51% of owners see AI as inappropriate likely reflects scepticism about the cost figures specifically, not the full range of tasks AI already handles well.

Can a free AI tool safely generate the cost figures for a tender?

Not without validation. Lockton's guidance warns that AI-generated cost figures used in a tender without senior quantity surveyor sign-off may breach professional indemnity insurance terms. Free tools are well suited to drafting and structuring work, less suited to unsupervised final numbers.

What is the biggest AI skills gap in UK construction right now?

CIOB's Skills Gap Index, based on responses from over 2,500 members, found 54% see a significant gap specifically in using AI, alongside 48% in digital technology more broadly, part of why cost-estimating use lags well behind admin and marketing use.

Which construction tasks are safest to use free AI tools for right now?

Administrative and drafting work: parsing specifications, extracting dimensions, structuring a preliminary quantity list, and drafting scope descriptions. These match the tasks Houzz's data shows already delivering measurable time savings, without the professional indemnity exposure of an unverified cost figure.

Why this matters for you

A small construction firm without a large QS team has the most to gain from the admin and drafting time savings Houzz measured, and the most to lose from a professional indemnity dispute over an unverified cost figure. Splitting the two uses, free AI for structure and drafting, a senior QS for the final number, lets a small firm capture the time savings without taking on the insurance risk.
