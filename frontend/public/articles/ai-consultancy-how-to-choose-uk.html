You've identified that your business needs AI help, you have a rough budget, and now you're facing a list of consultancies that all use the same words: transformation, strategic, bespoke. Almost none of that language predicts whether the engagement will actually work. A few specific, checkable things do.

MIT's research found that buying from a specialised vendor and implementing with support succeeds roughly 67% of the time, versus about a third as often for projects built entirely in-house. The consultancy you choose is effectively deciding which of those two outcomes you're closer to.

Ask for Evidence, Not Case Studies

The difference between a client logo and a working system

Almost every consultancy website has a case study section, and almost every case study describes an outcome without describing the starting conditions that made it achievable. Ask instead for the name of a client in a business roughly your size and complexity, and ask if you can speak to them directly. A consultancy confident in its work will arrange this without hesitation. One that hedges is telling you something.

Check How They Handle the First Failure

S&P Global found 46% of proofs of concept get scrapped. Ask what happens then

Research from S&P Global, surveying over 1,000 enterprises, found the average organisation scrapped 46% of its AI proofs of concept before reaching production. That's not a reason to avoid AI consultancy, it's a reason to ask a specific question during selection: what does this consultancy do when a pilot doesn't work? A good answer describes a decision point, with a clear metric and a named moment to scale, fix, or kill the project. A vague answer, or one that implies pilots always succeed, is a red flag.

Look for Workflow Fluency, Not Just AI Fluency

Gartner's data points to integration as the real differentiator

Among organisations that landed at least one successful AI use case, 77% credited two specific things: integrating the tool into an existing workflow, and securing full support from the people who run that workflow day to day. Neither of those is primarily a technical skill. During your evaluation, ask a candidate consultancy to describe, in detail, how they'd map one of your actual processes before recommending any tool. If they jump straight to a tool recommendation, that's a sign they're selling technology rather than solving your problem.

Compare Proposals on Structure, Not Just Price

A cheaper proposal with no defined metric is not a bargain

When comparing two or more proposals, check whether each one names a single success metric, a named owner on your side, and a defined decision point at the end of the pilot phase. A proposal missing all three of these, regardless of price, is more likely to become one of the abandoned proofs of concept than a working system.

Quick Answers

What business owners ask before hiring an AI consultancy

Should I choose a specialist boutique or a large consultancy?

It depends on how well-defined your use case already is. A well-scoped, narrow problem is often served well by a specialist boutique. A broad, ambiguous transformation effort benefits from a larger team with more delivery capacity, at a higher price.

How many consultancies should I get proposals from?

Three is usually enough to see genuine differences in approach without spending weeks in a procurement process. Compare their questions to you as closely as you compare their answers.

What's the biggest red flag during selection?

A consultancy that recommends a specific tool or platform before understanding your actual workflow. That sequence, tool first, process second, is exactly the pattern behind most failed pilots.

Is a free initial consultation worth attending?

Generally yes, and it's a useful test in itself. Notice whether they spend the session asking about your business or presenting their own capabilities.

Why this matters for you

A small business has less room to recover from a badly chosen consultancy partner than a large enterprise does. You likely won't get a second budget approval if the first engagement produces nothing measurable. That makes the evidence-based questions above more important for you, not less, because you're less likely to have a procurement team built to catch these issues before a contract is signed.
