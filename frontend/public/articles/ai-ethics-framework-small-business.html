What ethics, compliance and governance actually mean for you

Three words, one small business version

Ethics is the standard you hold yourself to even when nobody is checking. For a small business, this usually comes down to one plain question: would you be comfortable explaining this AI decision to the customer it affected? Compliance is the legal minimum. Under UK GDPR and the EU AI Act, this means proper consent for data use and, for a narrow band of high-risk applications, documented testing. Governance is the habit that ties the two together: writing down what you use AI for, checking it periodically, and having one person who owns that list.

You do not need all three to be perfect from day one. You need governance running, even in a simple form, because it is the part that catches problems in ethics or compliance before a regulator or a customer does.

The EU AI Act does apply to small firms, just not how people expect

What changed in 2026

The Act's transparency obligations, telling people when they are dealing with AI and labelling AI-generated content, take effect on 2 August 2026 regardless of company size. The stricter documentation and testing rules for high-risk systems, originally due the same date, were pushed back to December 2027 after EU negotiators agreed more time was needed. Firms with up to 750 staff and €150 million in revenue also get a simplified compliance track: standard templates, sandbox access, and fines calculated in proportion to company size rather than a flat cap.

In practice, a fifteen-person business using AI to draft marketing copy or triage support tickets has almost nothing to do under the Act right now beyond being upfront that AI is involved. A business using AI to screen job applicants or assess credit risk sits in a different category and should get advice before scaling that use.

Why bias testing is not only a big-company problem

What happens when nobody checks

The Mobley v. Workday case, certified as a nationwide collective action in May 2025, shows what happens when a screening tool nobody audited quietly filters out older applicants for years before anyone notices. The tool was not built by the businesses using it. They were still named as defendants. Under both UK and EU frameworks, using someone else's AI product does not move responsibility for its outcomes away from you.

The fix costs far less than the lawsuit. Before using any AI tool that scores, ranks or filters people, whether that is job applicants, loan applicants or customer complaints, run it against a handful of real historical cases where you already know the fair outcome. If the tool's answer does not match your own judgement, raise it with the vendor before relying on it further.

A one-page governance habit that actually gets used

What to write down

The ICO's guidance is clear that broad, one-line consent statements are no longer good enough. Fixing that does not require a legal team, just specificity. For each AI tool in use, note what it does, what data it touches, who owns the relationship, and when it was last checked. Four columns, one spreadsheet, reviewed quarterly.

This single document does three things a full governance committee would otherwise do. It gives you an answer when a customer asks how a decision was made. It gives you a paper trail if a regulator asks. And it forces someone in the business to actually look at each tool periodically, rather than assuming it still works as intended.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Do UK small businesses actually have to comply with the EU AI Act?

Only if you serve EU customers or operate in the EU. The transparency rules, telling people when AI is involved, apply from 2 August 2026 regardless of size. The heavier documentation rules for high-risk systems now apply from December 2027.

What counts as a high-risk AI use that needs extra care?

Anything that screens job applicants, assesses credit or insurance risk, or otherwise makes decisions with a real legal or financial effect on a person. Marketing copy, scheduling and support ticket triage do not fall into this category.

Do we need an ethics committee?

No. A single spreadsheet listing every AI tool in use, what it touches, who owns it, and when it was last checked covers most of what a committee would do for a business under roughly fifty people.

What does getting this wrong actually cost?

The Mobley v. Workday case and similar hiring-bias claims show legal exposure attaches to the business using the tool, not only the vendor who built it. Checking a tool against known-good outcomes before relying on it costs an afternoon. The alternative costs considerably more.

Why this matters for you

Ethics frameworks are almost always written with a bank's compliance department in mind: committees, named roles, five and six-figure budgets. A business with twenty staff does not need that structure to avoid the two failures that actually cause damage, an AI tool that treats people unfairly and no record of having checked. A one-page tool log and a habit of testing anything that screens or ranks people closes most of the real risk without new hires.
