The Solicitors Regulation Authority's Risk Outlook on AI in the legal market does not ban AI drafting tools. It treats AI-assisted work the same as any other work product: the solicitor remains personally responsible for the result, whether or not AI was involved in producing it. The Risk Outlook groups the main dangers under a small set of headings, hallucinated citations to cases or statutes that do not exist, embedded bias, and confidentiality risk, and it recommends that firms tell clients when AI has been used on their matter and how it operates. For a small firm without a compliance department, that expectation is easy to miss entirely.

The SRA itself notes that AI could be particularly beneficial for small firms without a full administrative team behind each solicitor, while flagging cost as the barrier that stops many of them from adopting it. The gap between those two observations is exactly where a small firm's opportunity sits.

What the SRA actually requires, in plain terms

No ban, but personal responsibility either way

The SRA does not treat AI as a special category requiring separate permission. It expects the same professional standards applied to any other piece of drafting, which means checking the output properly before it goes anywhere near a client, the same as you would check a trainee's first draft. The Risk Outlook's specific concern about hallucination is worth taking seriously: AI tools can produce confident, correctly formatted citations to cases or legislation that simply do not exist, and this has already caught out lawyers in reported cases who did not check before filing.

The transparency expectation is the part most small firms overlook. The SRA's guidance is that clients should know when AI is being used on their matter and roughly how it works. A single line in your engagement letter or client care pack covers this. Waiting until a client asks, or never mentioning it at all, is the version of this requirement most likely to cause a problem later.

The compliance step most small firms skip

Free AI tools and client data do not mix well

The ICO's expectations sit alongside the SRA's and are easy to miss because they come from a different regulator entirely. Before client data goes into a third-party AI drafting tool, the ICO's guidance points towards a Data Protection Impact Assessment and a Data Processing Agreement with the vendor, with Standard Contractual Clauses needed if the data is processed in the US. Free consumer tiers of the major AI chat tools generally do not come with a DPA that meets these requirements, which makes a genuinely free account a real risk the moment client documents go anywhere near it.

This is a paperwork problem, not a technology problem, and it is solvable in an afternoon: check whether your AI vendor offers a business or enterprise tier with a proper data processing agreement, and use that tier for anything involving client information, keeping free consumer accounts for genuinely non-confidential drafting only.

What actually fits a two-partner firm's budget

Enterprise legal AI platforms are not the only option

Legal AI pricing analysis from Clio found that tools aimed at solo practitioners and small firms typically sit between £50 and £200 a month, a genuinely different market from enterprise platforms such as Harvey or CoCounsel, which start around £500 per seat with annual commitments. A small firm comparing its options against enterprise pricing is benchmarking against the wrong tier entirely, and will either overspend or conclude, wrongly, that AI drafting tools are out of reach.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Does the SRA prohibit solicitors from using AI to draft documents?

No. The SRA's Risk Outlook treats AI-assisted drafting the same as any other work product, with the solicitor remaining personally responsible for checking and standing behind the result, regardless of whether AI was involved in producing it.

Do clients need to be told if AI was used on their matter?

The SRA's guidance recommends it. A short line in your engagement letter or client care documentation explaining that AI tools may assist with drafting, and how they are checked, satisfies this expectation for most small firms.

Is it safe to use a free AI chatbot for legal drafting involving client information?

Generally not, according to ICO expectations. Free consumer tiers of most major AI tools do not include the Data Processing Agreement terms GDPR requires, so client data should only go into a paid business or enterprise tier with a proper DPA in place.

How much does AI legal drafting software actually cost for a small firm?

Tools aimed at solo practitioners and small firms typically run £50 to £200 a month, according to Clio's 2026 pricing analysis, a different tier entirely from the £500-per-seat enterprise platforms used by larger firms.

Why this matters for you

A small or solo legal practice is exactly the setting where AI drafting tools help most, since there is rarely a paralegal or administrative team to absorb routine drafting work. The SRA has said as much directly. The two things that actually stop a small firm from adopting AI safely are not technical: a missing line in the client care letter, and an AI subscription without a proper data processing agreement behind it. Both are fixable without hiring anyone.
