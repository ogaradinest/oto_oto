What a language model is actually doing

Not a database, not a search engine

A large language model behaves less like a calculator, which gives the same correct answer every time, and more like a well-read colleague answering from memory in a meeting: fast, usually right, occasionally confident and wrong, never pausing to check a source before speaking. Underneath, it predicts the next word, over and over, based on patterns picked up from enormous amounts of text during training. It has not looked anything up. It is generating the words most likely to follow, given everything said so far.

This is why an LLM can draft a proposal or summarise a report with real skill, and can also state a wrong figure or a fabricated case reference with exactly the same fluent confidence it uses when right. Nothing about the tone of the answer tells you which one you are getting.

The number that explains the trust problem

A public leaderboard, updated monthly

The Vectara Hallucination Leaderboard gives every named AI model a document to summarise and checks whether the summary sticks strictly to facts actually in that document. Results last updated in May 2026 put the strongest models under 3%, mid-tier models commonly between 5% and 12%, and the weakest tracked models over 20%. The point worth taking is not which model currently sits top of the table, since that changes with every update. Hallucination is a real, measured behaviour, and it varies meaningfully between models, not just between AI in general and no AI at all.

Before trusting any AI tool with something that needs to be factually right, check whether that specific model has a track record on a similar task, rather than assuming newer or pricier automatically means more accurate.

Why pasting in a whole document rarely works well

The middle of a long input is the weak spot

Researchers at Stanford and Meta AI tested how well language models find a specific piece of information depending on where it sits inside a long passage. The result held across every model family tested: accuracy stayed strong when the relevant detail sat near the start or end of what was fed in, and dropped by more than 30% when that detail sat in the middle, even for models built to handle long inputs. A bigger context window does not fix this. It just moves where the weak spot sits.

The fix needs no technical knowledge: when feeding a contract, email chain, or long report into an AI tool, put the specific question or most important fact near the top, or break a long document into shorter sections and ask about each separately, rather than trusting the model to spot one detail buried on page forty of sixty.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

What is a large language model, in one plain sentence?

Software trained to predict the next word based on patterns learned from enormous amounts of text. It is not a system that looks up or verifies facts, so it can produce fluent, confident answers that are sometimes wrong.

How many UK businesses are actually using AI tools like this?

The ONS found 25% of UK businesses reported using some form of AI by December 2025, up 15 percentage points since the question was first tracked in September 2023. Most businesses have not deployed anything yet, so there is no reason to feel behind.

How can I check whether an AI model is likely to make things up?

The Vectara Hallucination Leaderboard publishes a monthly, independently run comparison of how often named models invent information not in the source they were given. Rates currently range from under 3% for the strongest performers to over 20% for the weakest, so the model you pick genuinely matters.

Why did an AI tool miss something I know was in the document I gave it?

Peer-reviewed research from Stanford and Meta AI found accuracy drops by more than 30% when the relevant detail sits in the middle of a long document rather than near the start or end. Putting key information near the top, or splitting a long document into shorter sections, works around this.

Why this matters for you

A small business without an in-house AI specialist is the most likely to either over-trust an AI tool's output, by pasting in a contract and accepting a summary without checking a clause buried in the middle, or avoid the tools altogether out of a vague sense that they just guess. Knowing two specific, measured things, that hallucination rates differ meaningfully between named models and that long documents have a genuine weak spot in the middle, turns an AI tool into something with known limits rather than a mystery box.
