The Number That Actually Matters

Adoption is nearly universal. Impact isn't.

McKinsey's State of AI research found that 88% of organisations use AI in at least one business function. That number gets quoted constantly because it sounds like the argument is over. It isn't. Only 6% of those organisations qualify as high performers seeing significant bottom-line impact, and just 39% report any EBIT impact at the enterprise level at all.

BCG's research tells the same story from a different angle: 60% of AI initiatives generate no material value despite continued investment, while only 5% create substantial value at scale. Adoption was never the hard part. Turning it into a result was.

The Three Questions That Matter

Answer these before you write a single line about tools or vendors

  • Which specific, repeatable task is costing us the most hours this quarter?
  • Who owns the outcome if the AI gets it wrong?
  • What does "good enough" look like, written down, before we start?

If you can't answer all three, you don't have a strategy yet. You have a wish list. This is where most engagements should start, and it's exactly what a proper first 90 days is built around.

Why Ownership Changes Everything

The one factor McKinsey found correlates most with real impact

Fewer than 30% of companies report that their CEO directly sponsors the AI agenda, according to McKinsey. That single factor, CEO ownership of AI governance, correlates more strongly with bottom-line impact than any other variable McKinsey measured.

There's a second pattern worth noticing. The organisations McKinsey classifies as high performers aren't starting with cost reduction. They're using AI for growth and innovation from the outset. A strategy built purely to trim headcount tends to produce exactly the underwhelming result that headcount-focused thinking predicts.

Strategy Without Implementation Is Theatre

A document that never gets built is worse than no strategy at all

A strategy document that never gets implemented creates the illusion of progress while nothing actually changes. It lets a business tell itself it has "done AI" without ever testing whether any of it holds up against real work, real data, and a real customer. The unglamorous part, the actual implementation checklist, is where the 88% either turns into the 6% or quietly joins the 60% that never delivers anything.

Quick Answers

What comes up before any AI strategy conversation

If almost everyone has adopted AI, why does strategy still matter?

Because adoption and impact are different things. 88% of organisations use AI somewhere, but only 6% see significant bottom-line impact, according to McKinsey. Strategy is what decides which group you end up in.

Does a small business really need CEO-level sponsorship?

At small business scale, that just means the owner or managing director has to actually own the decision, not delegate it entirely and check in occasionally. McKinsey's data links direct ownership to materially better outcomes at any size.

Should we lead with cost-cutting to justify the investment?

The organisations McKinsey classifies as high performers generally don't. They lead with growth and innovation use cases. Cost-cutting framing tends to produce small, easily-cancelled projects rather than lasting change.

What's the actual first step?

Answer the three questions above, in writing, before any tool or vendor conversation happens. If you can't answer them yet, that's the actual starting point, not a shortcoming to work around.

Why this matters for you

A large enterprise can afford to be part of the 60% that sees no material value this year and try again next year. A small business usually gets one real attempt before the team stops believing in the next one. Getting the strategy question right before you spend anything matters more for you, not less.
