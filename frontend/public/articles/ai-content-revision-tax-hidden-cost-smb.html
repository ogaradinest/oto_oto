A 2026 global study from Optimizely, conducted by Savanta and covering 2,003 marketing leaders across the UK and six other countries, found that 76% of marketers spend at least three hours a week editing, fact-checking, or correcting AI-generated output. Optimizely calls this the revision tax: the hidden time cost of turning a fast AI draft into something safe to publish. 48% named fact-checking and hallucination review as the single biggest source of that extra work, and 40% said they lose time moving content between disconnected tools. None of this means AI content is not worth using. It means the time it saves on drafting and the time it costs on checking are two separate numbers, and a business that only plans for the first one will feel like AI is not saving anything at all.

54% of marketers in the same study said leadership underestimates the human effort actually needed to make AI-generated work usable. In a small business, the owner is often both the leader with that expectation and the person doing the checking, which makes the gap easy to miss until the time is already gone.

The cost nobody budgets for

Faster drafting does not automatically mean faster publishing

AI genuinely does cut the time it takes to produce a first draft of a social post, an email, or a web page. What Optimizely's research shows is that this saved time frequently reappears somewhere else in the process, as checking, rewriting, and fixing before the content is fit to publish. 76% of marketers report spending three or more hours a week on exactly this kind of correction work, and fact-checking accounts for the largest share of it. The total time a piece of content takes has not necessarily gone down. It has moved from the writing stage to the review stage.

Why owners and staff experience this differently

The expectation gap is measured, not imagined

The same research found a sizeable gap between how senior leadership and delivery-level staff see AI's impact. 54% of marketers said leadership underestimates the real human effort needed to make AI output usable, and separately, 69% of C-suite respondents said AI adoption felt fully aligned across their organisation, against just 27% of analysts closer to the actual work. In a small business this split usually happens inside one person rather than between two teams. The part of you setting the expectation that AI will save hours is not always talking to the part of you doing the fact-checking at 9pm, and naming that gap is the first step to planning around it honestly.

Three ways to cut the revision tax without spending anything

Small changes to the process, not new software

Give the AI tool real detail before it drafts, not a one-line prompt: the actual audience, the specific claim being made, and one or two past examples of writing the business is happy with. A more specific starting point produces fewer wrong facts and less generic tone to fix afterward. Keep a short, fixed checklist for every AI-assisted piece rather than re-reading the whole thing from scratch each time: is every factual claim checked, does it sound like the business rather than like anyone, and does it say one thing worth saying. Finally, do the drafting and the review in the same place rather than passing content between a chat tool, a document, and a publishing platform, since 40% of the wasted time in the Optimizely study came specifically from moving content between disconnected systems.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

What is the 'revision tax' in AI marketing content?

It is the hidden time cost of checking, fact-checking, and rewriting AI-generated content before it is safe to publish. Optimizely's 2026 global study found 76% of marketers spend at least three hours a week on this kind of correction work.

Does this mean AI content tools are not actually saving time?

Not necessarily. AI usually does cut drafting time. The research shows that saved time is often spent instead on review and fact-checking, so the total time per piece of content may not fall unless the review process itself is also made more efficient.

Why do business owners and staff sometimes see AI's impact so differently?

Optimizely found 54% of marketers say leadership underestimates the real effort needed to make AI output usable, and a large gap between how aligned C-suite respondents and analysts felt AI adoption actually was. In a small business, that gap can exist within one person wearing both hats.

What is the cheapest way to reduce time spent fixing AI content?

Give the AI more specific detail upfront rather than a vague prompt, use one short fixed checklist instead of a full re-read each time, and keep drafting and review in the same tool rather than moving content between several disconnected systems.

Why this matters for you

A small business rarely has a separate team to absorb the checking time AI content actually requires, so the revision tax lands directly on the owner or the one person managing marketing. Planning for that time honestly, rather than expecting AI to remove it, is what keeps AI content genuinely useful instead of quietly exhausting.
