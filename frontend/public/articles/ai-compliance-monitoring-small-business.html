Full internal audit functions, quarterly committees, dedicated staff, six-figure platform contracts, are built for organisations well beyond most small businesses. Yet the pressure to show that controls actually work is not only an enterprise problem any more. The FRC's UK Corporate Governance Code now requires listed company boards to declare, from 1 January 2026, that their material internal controls are effective, and that expectation is filtering down through supply chains, lenders and insurers who increasingly ask smaller suppliers a similar question informally.

You do not need a continuous audit platform costing tens of thousands of pounds. You need to know which handful of processes actually carry risk, and a cheap, consistent way to check them more than once a year.

Why checking once a year is no longer good enough

What changed with the 2024 Governance Code

Provision 29 of the FRC's 2024 UK Corporate Governance Code asks boards to declare the effectiveness of their material internal controls, not just describe having them. This applies directly only to listed companies, but the practical effect reaches further. Banks assessing lending risk, private equity owners, and larger customers running supplier due diligence increasingly expect the same standard of evidence, even from businesses the Code does not technically cover.

The old model, an annual spot check of a small sample of transactions, was designed for a slower, simpler business. It cannot realistically demonstrate that controls work continuously, because it only looks at a handful of moments a year.

What continuous monitoring actually looks like without a team

You are checking patterns, not hiring auditors

Continuous monitoring tools do not require a dedicated audit department to run. Modern platforms connect to your accounting system, HR system or CRM and check every transaction against expected patterns automatically, flagging duplicate invoices, payments to newly-added bank accounts, or approvals that skip a step. A finance manager or owner reviews the flagged exceptions, typically a handful a week rather than hundreds of transactions.

Reported returns from moving to this model are significant: three-year ROI above 285% against traditional periodic audits, with payback typically inside 8 to 18 months once earlier fraud detection and labour hours saved are counted together.

Where outsourcing beats building

The £3,000-£5,000 comparison that matters

Hiring even a single internal audit specialist costs a small business a full salary plus overhead for a function that, at this scale, does not need daily attention. Outsourcing the function to a specialist firm typically runs £3,000 to £5,000 a year and also solves the independence problem: an outsourced reviewer is not checking their own manager's work.

Small businesses carry a structural disadvantage worth naming plainly: proportional compliance costs run roughly three times higher than for larger firms, because the fixed cost of understanding a framework does not shrink with headcount. Outsourcing spreads that fixed cost across a specialist firm's whole client base instead of carrying it alone.

If you are FCA-regulated, the bar is already higher

What the FCA actually wants to see

Businesses regulated by the FCA, including many providing consumer credit, insurance mediation or investment advice, already face a specific evidence standard for AI-assisted decisions under Consumer Duty. Current guidance points to five things: a risk assessment carried out before deployment, a named senior manager accountable for the system, real-time monitoring with a documented threshold for stepping in, an audit trail at the level of individual interactions, and evidence covering any third-party tools relied on.

None of this requires an audit department. It requires writing down, once, who owns each AI-assisted process and reviewing the flagged exceptions on a fixed schedule, then keeping that record somewhere a regulator or lender could actually find it.

Quick answers

The questions most founders ask first

Does the FRC Governance Code apply to our small business?

Directly, only if you are a listed company. In practice, banks, larger customers and private equity owners are increasingly asking smaller suppliers similar questions about control effectiveness, so it is worth having an answer ready regardless.

What is the cheapest way to start continuous monitoring?

Identify the two or three processes that actually carry financial or regulatory risk, usually supplier payments and payroll, and connect a monitoring tool or outsourced reviewer to just those first, rather than attempting company-wide coverage on day one.

Is outsourcing internal audit actually cheaper than hiring?

Usually, at small business scale. Typical outsourcing costs of £3,000 to £5,000 a year compare favourably to a full salary and overhead for a role that does not need daily attention below a certain size.

What does the FCA expect from regulated firms using AI?

Five things: a pre-deployment risk assessment, a named accountable senior manager, real-time monitoring with documented thresholds, an audit trail at the interaction level, and evidence covering any third-party AI tools in use.

Why this matters for you

A twenty-person business will never need a four-person audit team. It does need to know that supplier payments and payroll, the two areas where fraud and error actually cost money, are being checked more often than once a year. Outsourcing that check for a few thousand pounds annually, or running a lightweight monitoring tool against just those processes, closes most of the real risk that the FRC's new control-effectiveness standard is aimed at, without hiring anyone.
