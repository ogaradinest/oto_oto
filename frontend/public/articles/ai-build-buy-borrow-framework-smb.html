Build, buy, or borrow: the third option most advice skips

KPMG's framework, translated down

KPMG's framework treats building as the path for full control and deep differentiation, at the cost of the most time, money and in-house expertise. Buying means fast access to a proven, prebuilt tool, at the cost of looking like every other business using the same product. Borrowing sits in between: co-developing something with a specialist partner who brings the technical capability while you bring the domain knowledge of your own business, sharing both the cost and the risk of getting it wrong.

For a small business, borrowing usually looks like working with a consultancy or development partner who builds something genuinely shaped around your workflow using existing frameworks and models, rather than a fully bespoke build from zero or a generic subscription that almost fits. It sits closer to buying in cost and speed, and closer to building in how well the result actually matches your business, which is exactly why most binary build-or-buy guides miss it.

Build: full control, highest cost and time Buy: fastest, least differentiated Borrow: shared cost, shaped around your workflow

The one number that applies no matter which path you pick

The ongoing cost, not the upfront one

Annual maintenance, covering model retraining, performance monitoring, and updates as the systems around it change, typically runs 15 to 25% of the original build cost every year, regardless of how the system was created. Building it yourself means your business carries all of that cost directly. Buying an off-the-shelf tool usually bundles most of it into the subscription price, so the vendor absorbs it instead. Borrowing through a partner typically splits it: the partner handles the technical maintenance under an ongoing support arrangement, while you pay a smaller recurring fee than a full in-house team would cost.

This is the number most small businesses forget to ask about before choosing a path. A build that looks affordable based on the initial quote can become the most expensive option within two years once that annual maintenance share is added in.

Three questions that place you on the decision tree

Adapted from KPMG's framework for a business with no dedicated tech team

Does an existing off-the-shelf tool already cover most of what you actually need, with only minor gaps? If yes, buy, and stop there. Is this specific capability the thing that genuinely differentiates your business from competitors, not just a nice-to-have efficiency gain? If yes, and you have the budget and time for ongoing maintenance, building may be worth it. If neither of those two answers is a clear yes, borrowing is usually the right default: a partner-built solution shaped around your business, without the full cost and risk of building entirely alone.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

What is the difference between buying and borrowing an AI solution?

Buying means using an off-the-shelf tool as it comes, with minimal changes. Borrowing, in KPMG's framework, means co-developing something with a specialist partner who combines their technical capability with your knowledge of your own business, producing a result shaped around your workflow without the full cost of building entirely from scratch.

Is building an AI solution in-house realistic for a small business?

Rarely, according to KPMG's own guidance, which describes building as best suited to organisations with advanced technical expertise, robust infrastructure, and continuous access to subject matter experts, generally larger, better-resourced companies. Most small businesses are better served by buying or borrowing.

Why does the build, buy, or borrow choice matter if the maintenance cost is the same either way?

The maintenance cost, typically 15 to 25% of build cost annually, applies on every path. What the decision actually changes is who carries it. Building means your business absorbs it directly, buying bundles it into a subscription, and borrowing usually splits it between you and your partner.

How is a blended build-and-buy approach different from picking one path?

KPMG's research found 57% of organisations now favour blending paths, for example buying a base platform and extending it with custom elements. For a small business, this often looks like buying a mainstream tool and borrowing a partner's help to configure or extend it for a specific workflow, rather than treating build, buy and borrow as mutually exclusive.

Why this matters for you

Most build-versus-buy guides are written for organisations with a technology department capable of actually building something, which leaves a small business choosing between an expensive, unrealistic option and a generic tool that never quite fits. KPMG's third path, borrowing through a specialist partner, is the one most guides skip, and it is usually the most realistic option for a business with no in-house AI team but a genuinely specific workflow a generic tool cannot match.
