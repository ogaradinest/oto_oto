Why the cost of getting this wrong is larger than it looks

A decade-long drag, not a one-off bad quarter

McKinsey research puts the cumulative cost of supply chain disruption at 45% of profit over a decade for the average business, a figure built from repeated smaller disruptions, a late shipment here, a stockout there, an emergency reorder at a worse price, compounding over years rather than one dramatic event. This scale of cumulative loss is exactly why even a partial improvement in forecasting accuracy or procurement discipline compounds into a meaningful number over time.

Procurement teams are already responding: 94% of procurement executives now use generative AI tools at least weekly, up 44 percentage points in a single year, and 94% of supply chain companies overall plan to use AI or generative AI for decision support within two years, with demand forecasting the single most common planned use case, cited by 91% of those firms.

What AI forecasting and procurement tools actually deliver

Fewer wrong guesses about what you will need and when

AI-powered demand forecasting reduces forecast errors by 20 to 50% compared with traditional methods, by analysing historical order patterns, seasonal trends and external signals like supplier lead time changes to predict demand more accurately than a manual spreadsheet-based estimate. Reported outcomes at businesses using AI across their operations include 5 to 20% lower logistics costs, 20 to 30% reductions in inventory levels, and 5 to 15% lower procurement spend, each driven by ordering closer to actual need rather than padding for uncertainty.

These figures compound with each other rather than standing alone: better forecasting reduces the safety stock a business needs to hold, which reduces inventory cost, which frees cash that would otherwise sit in a warehouse waiting for a forecast to be wrong.

The barrier that stops most small operations from getting here

Data quality, not appetite for the technology

61% of supply chain decision-makers cite poor data quality and system integration as the primary barrier to successful AI implementation, a challenge that disproportionately affects smaller operations without a dedicated IT resource to clean and connect supplier, order and inventory data before a forecasting tool can use it well.

This is a solvable problem, but it is a real one: an AI forecasting tool pointed at inconsistent supplier codes, incomplete order histories, or inventory counts that have not been reconciled in months will produce a confidently wrong forecast rather than a useful one. The work of tidying this data up is less exciting than the AI tool itself, but it is the actual prerequisite for any of the numbers above.

A realistic starting point for a smaller operation

Fix the data for your highest-volume suppliers first

Rather than attempting to clean and connect data across every supplier and product line at once, start with your highest-volume or highest-cost suppliers, the ones where a forecasting improvement would move the most money. Reconcile their order histories and current inventory counts into a single consistent format before introducing any AI forecasting tool to that subset.

Once a forecasting tool is running well on that smaller, cleaner dataset, measure the actual reduction in forecast error and stockouts against your own baseline before expanding to the rest of your supplier base. This gives you a real number from your own operation to weigh against the 20 to 50% industry range, rather than assuming your results will match the average.
