Say you've started running CVs through an AI tool because the applications are piling up and you don't have an HR team to lean on. Fair enough. But most owners assume this makes hiring more objective, less gut-feel, less "I just liked them." The research says the opposite can happen, it happens fast, and almost nobody catches it while it's happening.

When an AI tool shows a preference for one candidate over another, people go along with it up to 90% of the time, even when that preference is biased.

The Myth: AI Removes the Bias, It Doesn't Add It

People trust the machine's judgement more than their own

Take Marcus, who runs an 11-person logistics firm in Luton. He started running CVs through a free AI screening add-on last spring, drowning in applications for one warehouse supervisor role. It worked exactly as advertised, ranking 60 CVs down to 8 in minutes. What nobody had built it to flag was that three strong candidates with non-Anglicised names scored lower than near-identical CVs with Anglicised names. He only caught it because his co-founder recognised one of the filtered names from a trade event and asked why she hadn't made the cut.

That's not a one-off. The University of Washington study found that once an AI tool showed a preference for one racial group, reviewers followed it up to 90% of the time, even though the same people split their picks evenly with no AI recommendation shown at all. A separate audit of resume-screening tools found they favoured white-coded names in 85.1% of bias tests. The tool doesn't just reflect bias, it launders it, and people trust the output precisely because it looks objective.

The Fix: Treat the Score as a First Pass, Never the Final Word

Three habits that cost nothing and take minutes

You don't need to rip out your AI tool. You need a human check with actual teeth, not a rubber stamp. Strip names, schools and photos before the first pass if your tool allows it, most do via a settings toggle. Score against a short written list of what the job needs, not a vague "fit" rating, because that's exactly where bias hides easiest.

The CIPD survey of 814 UK bosses found a net 67.7% uncomfortable using AI to shortlist candidates, yet most had no process for checking it, which is the real problem. Discomfort without a check changes nothing. Once a month, pull a sample of rejected CVs and read five yourself. If the same pattern shows up twice, recalibrate the tool, or drop it, for that role.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Does this mean I shouldn't use AI to screen CVs at all?

No, just don't let it make the final call alone. Use it for volume, then spot-check what it filters out.

How do I know if my AI hiring tool is actually biased?

You can't audit the model, but you can audit the outcomes. Pull rejected CVs monthly and check whether a pattern keeps showing up.

Is this a real legal risk for a small business?

Yes. Discrimination law looks at the outcome, not who made the call. A biased result is your liability, tool or no tool.

What's the cheapest way to cut this risk this week?

Strip candidate names before any screening pass and swap a gut-feel summary for a numbered scorecard. Both are free.

Why this matters for you

A small business doesn't have a legal team to catch this before it becomes a problem, and one discrimination claim or one quietly-lost great hire hits a ten-person firm far harder than a ten-thousand-person one. The fixes here aren't expensive software, they're five-minute habits, which makes them one of the few AI risks a small business can actually afford to manage properly.
