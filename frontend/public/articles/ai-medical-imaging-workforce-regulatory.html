The scale of the workforce gap driving this conversation

A shortage that is getting worse, not better

Demand for CT and MRI imaging grew by 8% in 2024, while the radiology workforce grew by only 4.7% over the same period, widening an already significant gap. The Royal College of Radiologists puts the current shortfall at 29%, rising to a projected 39% by 2029 if nothing changes, and calculates the NHS needs 1,953 additional clinical radiology consultants to meet demand safely.

The consequence is measurable in patient wait times: nearly 940,900 scan results in England took longer than a month to reach patients in 2025, against an NHS target that no patient should wait beyond that. The financial cost of managing this gap with temporary measures, locums, overtime and outsourcing, reached £362 million in 2025 alone, and £1.4 billion over the last five years.

What MHRA's regulatory pathway actually looks like in 2026

A framework built specifically for this category of device

The MHRA has confirmed it will publish a dedicated regulatory framework for AI as a medical device during 2026, with an international reliance pathway expected to open in the first half of the year. This pathway is designed to let products already approved by trusted overseas regulators reach the UK market faster, potentially cutting six to twelve months off the usual approval timeline for eligible products.

The MHRA's AI Airlock regulatory sandbox, now moving into its third phase after securing renewed multi-year funding, has specifically explored large language models in clinical decision support and synthetic data for radiology validation, indicating imaging is a priority category for the regulator rather than an afterthought.

What this means for a smaller imaging provider or practice

Procurement and workforce planning, not a clinical decision

For a private diagnostic imaging provider or a smaller radiology practice, the workforce shortage translates directly into either longer waiting lists or higher spending on locum and overtime cover, the same pressure the NHS is managing at a larger scale. Any AI-assisted imaging tool under consideration should have a clear, current MHRA approval status, which a supplier should be able to state plainly and in writing.

The decision to adopt any specific tool, and how its output is used within a diagnostic pathway, is a clinical governance question for radiologists and the practice's clinical leadership, working within MHRA approval status and the guidance of relevant professional bodies. The business case for exploring this category of technology rests on the scale of the workforce pressure described above, not on any performance claim this article could responsibly make.

Questions worth asking before any procurement conversation

What a supplier should be able to answer clearly

What is the tool's current MHRA approval status, and under which specific use case was that approval granted. Has the tool gone through the international reliance pathway or a comparable route, and what evidence supports its intended use. What does the vendor say about how the tool fits into an existing reporting workflow, and who remains clinically accountable for the final report.

A supplier unable to answer these questions clearly and in writing is not ready for a procurement conversation, regardless of how the underlying technology performs, because the accountability and regulatory questions matter as much as the tool itself in a clinical imaging context.
