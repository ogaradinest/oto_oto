Why the gap between expectation and result is so large

The trough of disillusionment, in real numbers

Companies expect an average 171% return on investment from agentic AI, but only 39% can currently point to any actual profit impact from it. 60% of AI initiatives generate no material value despite continued investment, and only 5% create substantial value at scale. MIT research found that 95% of AI pilots deliver zero measurable impact on profit and loss. Generative AI itself has moved into what Gartner's 2026 Hype Cycle calls the trough of disillusionment, having passed the peak of inflated expectations back in 2024.

Agentic AI, AI systems that take multi-step actions rather than just answering a question, currently sits at the peak of its own hype cycle, and Gartner projects more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by 2027 due to unclear ROI, escalating costs and inadequate controls, suggesting this specific category is heading toward the same disillusionment curve generative AI has already been through.

A concrete example of how vendor claims diverge from reality

Cherry-picked case studies versus cross-program averages

One customer service AI vendor advertises an 80% ticket deflection rate. Independent data on Zendesk's enterprise customer base puts the real median deflection rate at 41.2%, a gap of 38.8 percentage points between the headline marketing figure and what a typical enterprise customer actually experiences. This is not necessarily a fabricated number, it likely reflects a genuine best-case customer, but presenting it as a representative expectation rather than a ceiling is where the claim becomes misleading.

This pattern, a genuine but exceptional result presented as a typical outcome, is common enough across the AI vendor landscape that any headline performance figure should be treated as the best case a vendor has seen, not the number your business should expect on day one.

The claims regulators are now actively pursuing

AI washing has moved from a concern to an enforcement priority

The FTC has filed 13 AI-washing enforcement cases against companies since 2024, targeting three specific patterns: claiming a product is AI-powered when it actually relies on simple rules or human work behind the scenes, exaggerating how advanced or proprietary the underlying AI actually is, and implying AI drives a specific result it does not actually produce. Regulators are applying existing fraud and deception law to these claims rather than waiting for new AI-specific statutes.

For a business evaluating a vendor, this regulatory activity is a useful signal in itself: a vendor facing scrutiny for exactly the kind of claim being made to you is a stronger warning sign than any due diligence question you could ask directly, and checking whether a vendor or its specific claims have faced regulatory attention is now a reasonable part of evaluating an AI purchase.

How to evaluate a vendor claim without the marketing gloss

Ask for the median, not the headline

Ask any vendor quoting an impressive headline figure for the median result across their full customer base, not their best case, and ask specifically how that median was measured and over what period. A vendor unwilling or unable to provide this is telling you something important about how representative their headline number actually is.

Treat any vendor's number as a ceiling to test against in your own pilot, not a guarantee, and measure your own result against your own defined metric over a fixed test window rather than assuming your business will match the marketing figure. The gap between 80% and 41% in customer service deflection shows exactly how large that difference can be.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Why do so few companies see the AI ROI they expect?

Companies expect an average 171% ROI from agentic AI, but only 39% currently show any actual profit impact, and MIT found 95% of AI pilots deliver zero measurable financial impact, largely because headline vendor figures reflect best cases rather than typical results.

How big is the gap between vendor marketing claims and real results?

It varies, but one documented example shows an 80% headline deflection rate claim against a real enterprise median of 41.2%, a gap of 38.8 percentage points, illustrating how a genuine best-case result can be presented as a typical outcome.

Are regulators actually taking action against exaggerated AI claims?

Yes. The FTC has filed 13 AI-washing enforcement cases against companies since 2024, targeting false claims about a product being AI-powered, exaggerating how advanced the AI is, or implying it produces results it does not.

What is the single best question to ask a vendor quoting an impressive AI statistic?

Ask for the median result across their full customer base, not their best case, and how it was measured. A vendor who cannot answer this clearly is likely quoting an exceptional result as if it were typical.

Why this matters for you

A small business has less time and budget to recover from a disappointing AI purchase than a large enterprise absorbing the same 95% pilot failure rate MIT identified. Asking a vendor for their median result rather than their headline figure, and checking whether regulators have scrutinised similar claims from that vendor or category, costs nothing and directly targets the specific gap between marketing and reality that trips up most AI purchases.
