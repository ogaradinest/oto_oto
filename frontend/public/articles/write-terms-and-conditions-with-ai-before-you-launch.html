List what could actually go wrong first

Your terms exist to answer arguments you haven't had yet

Before you open any AI tool, write down every way a transaction with your business could go sideways: a customer wants a refund you don't offer, a delivery runs late, someone resells what they bought, or a client claims you owe more work than you quoted for.

This list, not a generic template, is what makes your terms actually protect you. A kitchen-table candle business needs different clauses to a freelance web developer billing by the project, and a template pulled off Google covers neither of them properly.

Draft with AI, then check it against the real gaps

A generator or a general AI tool both work, if you feed them specifics

Feed your scenario list into ChatGPT, Claude, or a dedicated generator like Termly or Rocket Lawyer, naming your actual payment terms, delivery timelines, refund policy, and what happens if a client cancels partway through. Either route gets you a workable first draft in minutes, not the days a solicitor's queue usually takes.

Then check the draft covers what UK law actually expects: clear pricing, your right to change terms, a limitation of liability, and, if you sell to consumers online, the statutory 14-day cancellation right under the Consumer Contracts Regulations. AI is fast at structure and plain English; it doesn't know your real refund window unless you tell it.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Do I legally need terms and conditions before I launch?

There's no single law forcing every business to publish T&Cs, but UK consumer law already governs refunds, delivery, and cancellation rights regardless. Writing your own terms sets clear expectations up front, rather than leaving everything to statutory rules a customer may not know apply.

Can an AI-drafted terms and conditions document actually hold up?

As a solid baseline, yes, provided you feed it specific details about how you trade and check it covers UK basics like the 14-day cancellation right for online consumer sales. For anything unusual, like subscriptions or regulated goods, it's worth a solicitor's eye on the final draft.

What's the real difference between terms and conditions and a privacy policy?

Terms and conditions cover the rules of buying from you: pricing, delivery, refunds, liability. A privacy policy covers what personal data you collect and why. Most new businesses need both, as separate documents.

How often should I update my terms and conditions?

Review them whenever you change how you trade, such as a new delivery method, refund policy, or adding subscriptions, and at least once a year regardless, since UK consumer law itself gets updated periodically.
