The gap between AI use and AI policy

Staff are not waiting for permission

Only 31% of UK employers have a formal AI policy in place, and 25% of UK organisations still prohibit generative AI outright, yet staff use AI tools daily regardless of what any policy says. One in three UK employees has entered proprietary, confidential or sensitive company information into a public AI tool, and 16% have used an AI product explicitly banned by their own employer.

The consequences are measurable: unsanctioned AI use tripled to feature in 45% of breaches analysed in the 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, and two-thirds of C-suite leaders believe their organisation has already suffered a data leak or security breach caused by an employee using an unapproved AI tool. Only 24% of UK businesses using AI have any practices in place to manage this specific risk.

What a usage policy needs to cover, in plain terms

Specific tools, specific data, specific consequences

A workable staff-facing AI usage policy answers three questions clearly: which AI tools are approved for use, what categories of information must never be entered into any AI tool regardless of whether it is approved, and what a staff member should do if they realise they have made a mistake. Vague guidance like "use AI responsibly" gives staff nothing concrete to follow and explains why so many policies exist on paper without changing behaviour.

The categories of information worth naming explicitly are the ones that cause the most damage if leaked: customer personal data, financial figures not yet public, anything covered by an NDA, and login credentials or access details for any system. Naming these specifically, rather than referring generally to "sensitive information", removes the ambiguity that leads a well-meaning employee to paste something they should not have.

Why training matters as much as the document itself

A policy nobody has seen is not a policy

Only around 22% of UK businesses have provided AI-specific governance training to staff involved in AI deployment, and just 35% of UK employers provided any AI training or support at all. A policy document sitting unread in a shared drive does not change the behaviour behind the 45% of breaches linked to unsanctioned AI use.

A short session walking staff through the specific approved tools, the specific categories of information to avoid, and a real example of what a mistake looks like does more to change behaviour than the document alone, and takes far less time to organise than most businesses assume.

A realistic starting point for a small business

A one-page policy beats no policy at all

A usage policy does not need to be a lengthy legal document to be effective. A single page naming the approved tools, the categories of information never to enter into any AI tool, and a clear point of contact for questions or mistakes covers the ground that actually matters, and is far more likely to be read and followed than a long policy modelled on enterprise governance documents.

Being able to tell a customer or partner your business has a documented AI usage policy, uses only approved tools, and reviews AI-generated output before it goes external is a real point of difference, since most small businesses currently cannot say the same.

Why this matters for you

A small business has just as much exposure to the 45% of breaches linked to unsanctioned AI use as a larger organisation, but usually without a dedicated security team to catch a mistake before it causes damage. A short, specific, one-page usage policy naming approved tools and forbidden categories of information, paired with a brief staff walkthrough, closes most of this gap without requiring the lengthy governance documents larger organisations use.
