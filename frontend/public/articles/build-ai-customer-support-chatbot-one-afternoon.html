Start with your real questions, not a generic bot

The bot is only as good as what you feed it

Before touching any software, write down the 10 to 15 questions you already know you'll get: delivery times, returns, sizing, whether you ship to Ireland. Most founders skip this and dump their whole website into a chatbot on day one, which is how you end up with a bot that sounds confident and answers wrong.

Picture a new candle subscription business, Wick & Co. Before launch, the founder listed 12 likely questions — scent strength, allergy info, shipping windows, cancellation policy — and used only those as the bot's knowledge base. In its first week live, it handled roughly 35 of 50 customer messages without any human input.

Pick a no-code platform and connect it

You don't need to write a line of code

No-code platforms such as Tidio, Chatbase, and Crisp let you paste in a document or FAQ page and go live the same day, usually for £20 to £30 a month. Sign up, upload your question list as a knowledge base, embed the widget with a copy-paste snippet, and set a welcome message that matches how you actually talk to customers.

Then test it yourself, as a stranger would. Ask it three trick questions. If it guesses instead of admitting it doesn't know, tighten the knowledge base before you go live, not after.

Set the handoff rule before you go live

Decide upfront when the bot says "let me get a human"

Zendesk's research found 85% of CX leaders say one unresolved issue is enough to lose a customer, so the handoff matters more than the bot's cleverness. Set a clear rule: after two failed attempts, or for anything about refunds or an order gone wrong, the bot hands off to your email or WhatsApp immediately, rather than looping.

In the UK, it's also good practice to tell customers upfront they're chatting with AI. A one-line disclosure in the welcome message costs nothing and avoids the trust hit of someone finding out later.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

How much does an AI chatbot cost for a brand-new business?

Most no-code platforms have a small-business tier around £20–£30 a month, with free trials to test first. Usually cheaper than a few hours of your own time answering the same questions.

Will customers know they're talking to a bot?

They should. A short line in the welcome message stating it's an AI assistant, with an easy route to a human, is both good practice and increasingly what UK customers expect.

What if the bot gets something wrong?

Ground it only in the FAQ and policy documents you upload, not open web answers, and check its transcript weekly for the first month. Most errors trace back to a thin knowledge base, not the AI itself.

Do I need this before I even have customers?

Yes, if you're taking pre-orders, building a waitlist, or running paid ads — those all generate questions before launch day. Setting it up early means it's tested by the time real customers arrive.

Why this matters for you

For a business of one or two people, every hour spent typing "yes, we ship to Northern Ireland" for the fifth time is an hour not spent on product, suppliers, or the next sale. A chatbot doesn't replace the judgement calls only you can make, but it absorbs the repetitive ones automatically, at a cost that's a rounding error next to hiring help — and makes the business look responsive from day one.
