A Forrester Total Economic Impact study found that Google Workspace with Gemini delivers a 336% return on investment over three years, with 40% faster information retrieval and a 30% improvement in collaboration. A separate Forrester study on Microsoft 365 Copilot found a 116% three-year ROI, built around an average of 9 hours saved per user per month. Both studies were commissioned by the vendor, so the headline multipliers deserve some scepticism. What is harder to dismiss is the underlying pattern: a meaningful amount of that value comes from AI features already included in subscriptions many small businesses already hold.

McKinsey's State of AI research found that AI high performers are distinguished less by which tool they bought and more by whether they redesigned an actual workflow around it. Switching on a built-in AI feature changes nothing if the old manual step stays in place alongside it.

The AI you already pay for is probably switched off

Two commissioned studies, one honest caveat

Forrester's Microsoft 365 Copilot study is built on a composite organisation with $6.25 billion in annual revenue and 25,000 employees, so the absolute dollar figures do not translate directly to a small business. The underlying finding scales down more honestly than the headline number: real time savings, roughly 8 hours a month for typical users and considerably more for heavy users, come from replacing a specific repeated task, not from installing the feature and carrying on as before.

Google's Gemini-in-Workspace study reports similar shape: faster information retrieval and better collaboration, concentrated in the specific tasks people already do most often inside email, documents, and spreadsheets. A small business is unlikely to see 336% written on an invoice anywhere, but the direction of the finding, that a lot of value sits inside tools already being paid for, holds regardless of company size.

Why 'it is already there' does not mean 'it is already used'

Workflow redesign, not just a new button

McKinsey's research on AI high performers found that the businesses seeing real returns tend to redesign the specific workflow around the AI feature, rather than bolting the feature onto an unchanged process. A team that turns on an AI meeting summary tool but still has someone manually write up the same notes afterwards has added a step, not removed one. The feature only pays for itself once it actually replaces something.

This is the part most small businesses skip. Buying the subscription is easy. Sitting down and asking which specific weekly task, the meeting notes, the first-draft email reply, the report someone pulls together every Friday, an existing AI feature could fully take over, rather than just assist with, is the step that turns a paid-for feature into an actual time saving.

Three places to look before any custom integration

The order that avoids an unnecessary IT project

Start with the admin settings of whatever tool you already pay the most for, email and document platform, CRM, accounting software, and check what AI features are included but not switched on. Most subscription software has added at least one in the past year. Next, look at simple, no-code automation between the tools you already use; connecting two existing tools with a native AI step rarely needs custom development. Only once both of those are genuinely exhausted does a custom integration project, with its own separate cost and timeline, become worth pricing out.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Do the Copilot and Gemini ROI figures apply to a small business?

Not literally. Forrester's Microsoft 365 Copilot study is modelled on a 25,000-employee composite organisation, so the dollar figures do not scale down directly. The underlying pattern, that real value comes from replacing a specific task rather than adding the feature on top, applies regardless of company size.

Should a small business turn on every AI feature in its existing software?

No. Pick the one weekly task that already takes the most time and check whether an existing feature can fully replace it, rather than switching on every available feature at once and hoping something sticks.

When does a custom AI integration actually make sense?

Only after checking what your existing subscriptions already include and what simple no-code automation between tools can achieve. A custom integration project carries its own separate cost and timeline, and is rarely the right first step.

Why do businesses pay for AI features they never use?

Usually because switching the feature on was treated as the finish line rather than the start. McKinsey's research found the businesses seeing real returns redesign the actual workflow around the feature, removing a manual step rather than simply adding a new one alongside it.

Why this matters for you

A small business is more likely than a large enterprise to already be paying for Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, or a CRM with AI features bundled in and untouched, since it rarely has a dedicated team whose job is to audit what a subscription actually includes. Checking existing tools before buying anything new, or before pricing a custom integration, is the cheapest possible AI project a small business can run, because most of the cost was already paid months ago.
