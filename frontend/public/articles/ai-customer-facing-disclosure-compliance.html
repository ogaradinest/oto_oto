On 9 March 2026, the UK's Competition and Markets Authority published its first guidance on AI agents and consumer law, titled Complying with consumer law when using AI agents. The core message is simple: if a customer is talking to an AI system, they need to know it, and the business deploying that system stays fully responsible for what it says and does, even when the technology was built and supplied by someone else. The EU AI Act adds a second, more specific version of the same idea from 2 August 2026, requiring chatbots and virtual assistants to disclose they are AI at or before the first interaction.

The detail most small businesses miss: the CMA is explicit that you remain legally responsible for your AI agent's mistakes even when a third party built the tool. Buying a chatbot off the shelf does not transfer that responsibility to the vendor.

Two new rules, one requirement

Tell the customer, and mean it

The CMA's guidance is grounded in existing law, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 and the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, rather than a new AI-specific statute. Its central point is that the same consumer protection rules apply whether a customer deals with a human or an AI agent. Businesses are expected to clearly disclose when an interaction involves AI, particularly in situations where a customer might otherwise assume they are speaking to a person, and enforcement can carry fines of up to 10% of global annual turnover for serious breaches.

The EU AI Act's Article 50 covers similar ground with a sharper deadline. From 2 August 2026, providers of chatbots and virtual assistants must design them so a person is informed they are interacting with AI, with that notice given at or before the first interaction, not buried in terms and conditions afterwards. Fines run up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover. A UK small business without EU customers falls outside Article 50's direct scope. One that sells into the EU, or uses an AI vendor based there, should check its own position carefully, since that scope can apply sooner than expected.

What this actually looks like for a five-person business

Not a legal team, a sentence at the top of the chat window

For most small businesses, compliance is closer to a sentence than a project. A short line at the start of any AI-driven chat, email, or phone interaction stating that the customer is dealing with an AI assistant satisfies the core disclosure requirement in both the CMA guidance and the EU AI Act. Pair that with a visible, working way to reach a human, since the CMA's guidance treats a dead end with no escalation path as a consumer protection problem in its own right, not just a poor customer experience.

The second, easier-to-miss step is checking your chatbot or AI customer service vendor's contract for who carries liability if the tool gives a customer wrong information. Because the CMA has confirmed that responsibility sits with the business deploying the tool, not automatically with the vendor who built it, this is a five-minute contract check that most small businesses using an off-the-shelf tool have never actually done.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Does the CMA's AI agent guidance apply to a small business, not just large companies?

Yes. The guidance applies to any business using AI agents in consumer-facing roles, regardless of size, since it is based on existing consumer protection law rather than a size threshold.

Is a UK-only small business affected by the EU AI Act's chatbot rules?

Not directly, unless it sells into the EU or uses an AI vendor established there. However, the CMA's own guidance already imposes a very similar UK-side disclosure expectation, so most small businesses need to act on this regardless of the EU Act's scope.

Who is liable if a third-party chatbot gives a customer the wrong information?

The business that deployed it, according to the CMA's guidance, not automatically the vendor who built the tool. Checking your vendor contract for how liability is actually allocated between you is worth doing before an incident, not after.

What is the simplest way to comply with the new AI disclosure rules?

A short, clear line at the start of any AI-driven chat, email, or call stating the customer is talking to an AI assistant, plus a visible way to reach a human. Both the CMA guidance and the EU AI Act's Article 50 accept disclosure at this level of simplicity.

Why this matters for you

A small business is exactly the kind of organisation most likely to buy an off-the-shelf AI chatbot without a legal review of the contract behind it, and exactly the kind of organisation the CMA's guidance makes fully responsible regardless. The fix costs nothing: a disclosure line, a human escalation path, and a contract check. Doing all three takes under half an hour and removes the most likely source of a consumer law complaint from an AI tool a small business did not even build.
