A lot of advice tells small business owners that the "serious" way to do AI is to build something of your own: a custom chatbot, a bespoke little system nobody else has. It sounds like the grown-up choice, more control, no subscription creeping up every year. So plenty of owners quietly start building it themselves, or let a keen member of staff have a go, without ever really asking if that's the right call. Most of the time, it isn't.

Why building feels like the serious option

It looks like control. Often it's just extra hours.

Building something yourself feels like you're taking AI seriously, you're creating an asset instead of paying a subscription. That logic led the owner of a six-person joinery workshop near Milton Keynes to spend roughly three weekends building a quoting chatbot in a no-code tool, instead of paying around £40 a month for one already built for trades. It worked, eventually, but it ate about 25 hours of the owner's own time that weren't spent on actual jobs.

That's the cost almost nobody prices in. A finished, bought-in tool has already had its edge cases found and fixed by thousands of other users. A tool you build yourself hasn't, you're the one who finds them, usually at the worst moment.

What building it yourself actually costs you later

The risks show up after launch, not on day one.

The Pax8 data above isn't about big tech firms, it's small and mid-sized businesses. 35% found an AI tool or workflow running that nobody had formally signed off, often set up by a staff member solving their own problem in good faith. Without any review, that tool can end up handling customer data or quoting prices with nobody checking it's still right.

On top of that, over half the decision-makers in the same survey now spend three or more hours a week checking or fixing AI-generated output, time that eats straight into whatever the tool was supposed to save.

When building really is the right call

Only when it's the thing that actually makes you different.

KPMG's own guidance narrows it down well: build when the AI touches the one thing that genuinely sets your business apart, and buy for everything else. If your edge is a genuinely unusual way of pricing jobs or running your supply chain, owning that logic can be worth it. If you just need a chatbot or a quoting tool that any trade business could use, somebody has already built it, tested it on thousands of users, and priced it at a fraction of your time.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Isn't it cheaper long-term to build my own AI tool?

Usually not, once you count your own time. A £30-£50 a month tool that works on day one is almost always cheaper than the hours it takes to build and maintain something homemade.

What is "shadow AI" and why does it matter for a small business?

Any AI tool or workflow running in your business that you, the owner, never formally approved, often set up by a staff member solving their own problem. The risk is nobody's checking it's accurate or safe.

When does it make sense for a small business to build its own AI tool?

When the AI touches the specific thing that makes your business different: a unique pricing model or data no competitor has. For anything a dozen other businesses also need, an existing tool is almost always cheaper.

How do I know if a tool someone on my team built is safe to keep using?

Ask what data it touches, who can change it, and what happens if it's wrong. If you can't answer all three, that's your sign to formalise it or replace it with a reviewed, off-the-shelf tool.

Why this matters for you

For a small business, time is the real currency, not the subscription-fee comparisons AI blog posts love to run. Every hour an owner spends building and maintaining a homemade AI tool is an hour not spent serving customers, and unlike a subscription, that cost never shows up on an invoice.
