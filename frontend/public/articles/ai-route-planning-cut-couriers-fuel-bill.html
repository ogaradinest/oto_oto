The Whiteboard Routing Problem

Why planning routes by hand costs more than it looks like

Take a six-van same-day courier firm working out of Milton Keynes. Every morning, the owner spent 35 to 40 minutes building that day's routes by eye, using Google Maps and local knowledge of which roads jam up at 8am. It worked, mostly, but it was a guess dressed up as a plan.

The real cost showed up in the diesel bill: around £2,600 a month across the fleet, with vans regularly crossing each other's patch because the route wasn't built on actual traffic or drop density, just memory. A couple of late add-on jobs most weeks got bumped to the next day, because there was no clean way to slot them in without re-drawing the whole plan by hand.

None of this showed up as one dramatic number. It just leaked out, £30 here, an hour there, every day.

What Changed Once the Routes Got Smart

An AI route planner, not a bigger fleet

The owner started feeding the morning's drop list into an AI route-planning app instead. It builds a sequenced route per van in seconds, factoring in current traffic, delivery windows, and where each van starts from, rather than 35 to 40 minutes of guesswork.

Six weeks in, the fuel bill was down to roughly £2,000 a month, about a fifth lower, and rolled-over deliveries nearly disappeared. A last-minute drop just gets re-sequenced into the route instead of forcing a full re-plan by hand, and drivers get turn-by-turn on their phone, so the "where am I going next" calls mostly stopped too.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Do I need to replace the sat nav or app my drivers already use?

Usually not. Most AI route planners sit on top of what you've got, typically as an app the driver opens alongside their normal navigation, feeding it an optimised stop order rather than replacing it.

Is this only worth it for a big fleet?

No. Geotab's 2026 UK fleet data found small fleets of under 30 vehicles saw the biggest average fuel drop of any fleet size, bigger than mid-size or enterprise fleets.

What happens if a customer adds a last-minute drop?

Most tools re-optimise the remaining route in seconds rather than making the driver or dispatcher re-plan by hand, so one extra stop doesn't derail the whole day.

How long before it pays for itself?

It depends on fleet size and mileage, but most small operators see the fuel and time savings outweigh the subscription cost within a couple of months, since diesel is usually the single biggest controllable cost in a delivery business.

Why this matters for you

A small courier or delivery business doesn't have a logistics department. The person planning routes is usually the owner or one dispatcher, doing it before the first van even leaves the yard, on top of everything else they're responsible for that day. Shaving 15 to 20% off a fuel bill and cutting next-day rollovers isn't a nice-to-have gadget here, it's a direct line to the bottom line in a business where diesel is often the biggest cost after wages.
