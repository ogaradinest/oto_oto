A field experiment covering more than 6,200 customers, published in the journal Marketing Science, found that an undisclosed chatbot performed roughly as well as a proficient human sales worker, and about four times better than an inexperienced one. The same study found that disclosing the chatbot's identity before the conversation began cut purchase rates by more than 79.7%. The bot's actual competence had not changed. Customers who knew they were talking to a machine became curt and disengaged, perceiving it as less knowledgeable and less empathetic regardless. Since March 2026, the Competition and Markets Authority has told UK businesses to label an AI agent whenever the fact that it is not a person would affect a customer's decision. Those two facts sit in real tension, and the fix lives in tone and timing, not in hiding the bot.

The same research found the negative effect shrank with a later disclosure timing strategy and among customers who already had some experience dealing with AI. A business does not have to choose between staying compliant and keeping its bot effective, it has to choose when and how it says what it is.

Why Disclosure Hurts, Even When the Bot Is Good

It is a perception problem, not a competence problem

The mechanism the researchers found is specific: once customers know they are talking to a machine, they give shorter answers, disengage faster, and buy less, driven by a subjective sense that a disclosed bot is less knowledgeable and less empathetic, not by any actual drop in what it can do. That distinction matters for a small business, because it means the fix is not a smarter or more capable chatbot. The bot in the study was already performing at proficient-human level before disclosure. The problem sits entirely in how and when its identity gets revealed.

What UK Law Actually Requires

Honesty at the right moment, not silence

The CMA's March 2026 guidance asks businesses to consider labelling an AI agent so customers are not misled into thinking they are dealing with a real person, specifically when that fact would affect their decision, and not to overstate what the AI does or can do. It does not require every interaction to open with a flat announcement that the customer is talking to software. It requires that the fact is never hidden or misrepresented once it becomes relevant to what the customer is deciding.

The Tone Fix: Disclose Late, Confidently, and Clearly

Design choices that satisfy both the law and the data

Three practical adjustments follow directly from the research. Let the first exchange demonstrate real competence, answering the customer's actual question, before or alongside identifying itself, rather than opening with an announcement that it is a bot. State the AI involvement plainly and confidently once it becomes relevant, rather than apologetically, since an apologetic tone reinforces exactly the less-capable perception the study measured. Keep a visible, one-click route to a human agent at every stage, since that combination, not the absence of AI, is what both UK guidance and customer trust actually depend on.

Quick Answers

The questions small business owners ask most

Does telling a customer upfront that they are talking to a bot really hurt sales that much?

In the cited field experiment of over 6,200 customers, disclosing a chatbot's identity before the conversation began cut purchase rates by more than 79.7%, driven by how customers perceived the bot rather than any real change in its competence.

Do UK businesses legally have to disclose AI use in customer service?

Per CMA guidance published in March 2026, yes when the fact that it is AI rather than a person would affect a customer's decision. Businesses must not mislead customers into thinking they are speaking with a real person.

How can a business stay compliant without tanking its chatbot's performance?

Disclose at a natural point once the tool has already demonstrated it can help, keep the tone confident rather than apologetic, and always keep a live route to a human agent visible. The research found late disclosure meaningfully reduced the negative effect.

Does the type of task change how much disclosure hurts performance?

Yes. The original research found the negative disclosure effect was smaller with a later disclosure timing strategy and among customers who already had prior experience interacting with AI systems.

Why this matters for you

A small business chatbot is often the first, and sometimes only, contact point a prospective customer has, so getting the tone and timing of disclosure wrong costs real, measurable revenue on a channel the business already paid to set up. A large retailer can absorb inconsistent bot messaging across dozens of channels. A small business with one main chat entry point cannot afford an 80% drop-off on it.
