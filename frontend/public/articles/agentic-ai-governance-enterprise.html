Adoption Is Outrunning Control

The scale problem nobody budgeted for

Deloitte's 2026 survey of 3,235 business and technology leaders found that 74% of organisations plan to adopt agentic AI within two years — but only 21% currently have a governance model mature enough to manage the risks that come with it. That gap is widening, not closing, because the volume of agents in production is growing far faster than the controls around them.

IBM's Institute for Business Value puts a number on the scale: by the end of 2026, most large enterprises will be running a digital workforce of more than 1,600 AI agents, rising toward an average of 1,661 by 2027 — a 38% year-on-year increase. Only 11% of the technology executives IBM surveyed said they felt fully prepared to scale at that rate.

Gartner's forecast adds the counterweight: adoption of task-specific agents inside enterprise applications is set to jump from under 5% in 2025 to 40% by the end of 2026. Fast growth and thin governance rarely coexist for long.

Uniform Governance Doesn't Work

Why the same rulebook for every agent backfires both ways

Gartner's research, published in May 2026, is blunt about the most common governance mistake: applying identical controls to every AI agent regardless of what it actually does. Treat a low-risk scheduling agent the same as an autonomous agent with financial or customer-facing authority, and one of two things happens. Simple agents get buried in approval gates and delivery slows to a crawl. Genuinely autonomous, high-risk agents get under-restricted, and operational, security, and compliance exposure rises.

Gartner's conclusion is stark: more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027, driven by escalating costs, unclear business value, and — repeatedly — inadequate risk controls discovered only after deployment.

The fix isn't more governance. It's governance sized to risk. A practical starting model:

High Risk

Full human review

Before execution. Irreversible, financially material, or externally visible actions.

Medium Risk

Monitored execution

Agent acts, with real-time anomaly detection and a human in the loop on alert.

Low Risk

Autonomous with audit

Executes freely within constrained boundaries, reviewed periodically rather than in real time.

The Accountability Gap

Who is actually responsible when an agent acts?

IBM's Institute for Business Value surveyed 2,000 technology executives across 33 countries and 19 industries. Two-thirds said they are personally accountable for AI systems they don't fully control. IBM's own analysis of the accountability gap is direct about what that looks like in practice: after an incident, teams frequently cannot reconstruct what happened, why, or under whose authority — there is no audit trail showing which agent made which decision, using which data, at which point in the workflow.

There are signs the structure is catching up. 76% of organisations now report having a Chief AI Officer, up from just 26% in 2025 — a genuine governance signal. But a title is not a control system, and most organisations are still building the audit logging, identity management, and escalation paths that would let that role actually do its job.

Where Regulation Actually Stands

EU AI Act deadlines have moved. UK enforcement has started.

EU · Digital Omnibus, May 2026

High-risk deadlines pushed back

The Council, Parliament, and Commission reached a provisional deal deferring Annex III (use-based) high-risk obligations from August 2026 to December 2027, and Annex I (product-regulated) obligations from August 2027 to August 2028. Obligations on general-purpose AI models remain in force.

UK · No single AI Act

Enforcement through existing regulators

The UK governs AI through five existing regimes anchored by UK GDPR and the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025. In March 2026 the CMA confirmed agentic AI use must comply with existing consumer and competition law, flagging concerns on permissions, logging, and vendor lock-in.

The UK's ICO, CMA, Ofcom, and FCA have jointly formed a Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum hub for emerging technology — and picked agentic AI as its first priority area. The ICO is also drafting guidance on automated decision-making under the new Articles 22A–22D. Agentic AI remains the least settled corner of UK regulation in 2026, but it is no longer an unwatched one.

The questions enterprise leaders ask most

How many AI agents will a large enterprise run by the end of 2026?

Over 1,600 on average, according to IBM's Institute for Business Value — rising toward 1,661 by 2027, a 38% increase. Only 11% of technology executives say they feel fully prepared to scale at that pace.

Why does uniform governance fail for agentic AI?

Applying the same controls to every agent backfires in both directions — low-risk agents get over-restricted and delivery slows, while genuinely autonomous, high-risk agents get under-restricted and risk rises. Gartner links this directly to its forecast that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027.

What does the EU AI Act actually require right now?

General-purpose AI model obligations are already in force. The May 2026 Digital Omnibus agreement pushed the compliance deadline for high-risk, use-based systems from August 2026 to December 2027 — real breathing room, not a cancelled requirement.

Is there a "UK AI Act"?

No. The UK regulates AI through five existing regimes anchored by UK GDPR and the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, enforced by sector regulators. In 2026 the ICO, CMA, Ofcom, and FCA formed a joint hub specifically for agentic AI, and the CMA has already confirmed it falls under existing consumer and competition law.
