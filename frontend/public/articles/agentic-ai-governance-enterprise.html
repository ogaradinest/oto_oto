What Makes It Different

Agentic AI vs traditional AI vs generative AI

Traditional AI systems generate outputs — classifications, predictions, recommendations — that humans evaluate and act upon. Generative AI responds to prompts and produces content for human consumption. Neither independently accesses systems, invokes tools, or executes actions.

Agentic AI does all three. Where generative AI might draft an email, agentic AI can compose, review, revise, and send it — whilst learning from feedback to refine future behaviour. It pursues defined objectives with minimal continuous supervision, dynamically adjusting strategies in response to new information.

That capability gap is precisely what breaks conventional governance. The behaviour that creates risk emerges not from any single component but from the dynamic interaction between the agent's reasoning, tool selections, data flows, and integration points — the "invisible layer of risk."

The Risks

Four governance vulnerabilities unique to autonomous systems

Reasoning Opacity

Autonomous decisions happen across multiple reasoning steps with no single point of accountability. The "invisible layer" — emergent behaviour from tool interactions — is not captured in standard logs.

Excessive Agency

The OWASP Agentic Top 10 (2026) identifies this as critical: an agent faithfully pursues its objective within authorised tools but produces unintended consequences. A design failure, not a security breach.

Accountability Fragmentation

When agents delegate to other agents, accountability distributes across multiple decision points. "Invisible delegation" — agents acting under human credentials — erases the distinction between human and autonomous decisions.

Cascading Failures

Multiple agents making locally rational decisions can compound into system-wide drift. A demand forecast agent over-predicts → production scales up → inventory agents adjust → procurement negotiates new contracts. Each step correct; the outcome wrong.

The Regulatory Picture

What EU, UK, and NIST require from autonomous systems

EU AI Act

Effective Human Oversight

High-risk autonomous systems must enable humans to understand decisions, intervene before harm occurs, and maintain ultimate accountability. Compliance requires full documentation, testing, and audit trails.

UK Approach

Principles-Based Governance

The UK's AI Safety Institute takes a pro-innovation, context-based approach rather than prescriptive rules. Organisations demonstrate proportionate governance relative to risk, sector, and deployment context.

NIST (Mar 2026)

Identity-First Security

Autonomous agents require distinct identities separate from human users — least-privilege access, just-in-time credentials, and runtime behavioural drift monitoring. No human credential inheritance.

The Framework

Risk-proportionate oversight and immutable audit trails

Effective governance is not a blanket approval gate on everything an agent does. It is a tiered model based on the risk profile of each decision: financial impact, regulatory sensitivity, irreversibility, and potential for unintended consequences.

High Risk

Full human review

Before execution. Novel, irreversible, or externally visible decisions.

Medium Risk

Post-execution monitoring

Execute with real-time anomaly detection and human intervention on alert.

Low Risk

Autonomous with audit

Execute freely within constrained boundaries. Periodic human auditing.

Organisations implementing multi-tiered guardrails report 60% faster incident resolution and 80% reduction in false alerts.

Every agent interaction must be logged with:

  • Timestamp, agent identity, and the human authorisation that triggered it
  • Decision rationale and reasoning steps
  • Tools invoked and data sources accessed
  • Outcomes produced and downstream effects
  • Immutable storage — append-only, no retroactive alteration

Quick Answers

The questions enterprise leaders ask most

What is "excessive agency" and why does it matter?

It is when an agent faithfully pursues its objective within authorised tools but produces unintended consequences. It is a design failure — the agent did exactly what it was built to do, just not what the business intended. Governance must constrain not just which tools an agent can access, but what those tools can do in specific contexts.

Does the EU AI Act apply to our agentic systems?

If your systems make autonomous decisions affecting safety, financial outcomes, or regulatory compliance, yes — they fall within the high-risk category and must comply with documentation, testing, oversight, and audit requirements.

How do we balance autonomy with governance?

Classify decisions by autonomy level. Novel, ambiguous, or high-consequence decisions remain human-owned. Routine, constrained, reversible decisions can execute autonomously. The tier model above is a practical starting point.

What infrastructure do we actually need?

Four things: identity-first security controls for agents (distinct from human credentials), immutable audit logging, real-time anomaly detection, and documented escalation workflows that specify exactly who reviews what and when.
